# The Living Text World Overhaul — Backlog

Every requirement in the cumulative directive, numbered so it can be traced,
with its state in this tree. States mean exactly one thing each:

- **Done** — built, wired to a screen, tested, in this branch.
- **Partial** — built to a stated depth; the gap is written next to it.
- **Not started** — nothing in the tree yet.
- **Blocked** — cannot be finished here; the dependency is named.

Nothing is marked Done because its landing page works. The connected
scenario the directive asks for first is Done and walked in a browser; the
rest of the overhaul is not, and this file says where each piece stands.
Reconcile it against `docs/COVERAGE-MATRIX.md` and the command registry
before any delivery claim.

47. Life contract item 9, first part — the span, and what age lets you do. **"Just the full life cycle and everything a person goes through."**

    Nothing in the game aged. A character had a birth instant and every screen that wanted a number divided by 365.25 days; nobody had a birthday, nobody had a family they were born into, and `Person.aliveUntil` had sat on the model since the beginning with nothing anywhere setting it.

    **The family you were born into.** Two people who raised you, sharing your name — one of them keeps their own about half the time, because families work both ways — and however many brothers and sisters on a weighted draw, spaced the way brothers and sisters are, some of whom have moved somewhere else in the same country. Every age in the family is constrained from both ends: no parent who was a child at a birth, and none past having them. They are ordinary people in the world, so `rel.interact`, `rel.give_gift` and the rest already work on them, and they can be lost.

    **A birthday that happens, and an age counted properly.** Ageing is a ceiling rather than a cliff: what your best possible day looks like comes down continuously, held under by the daily rollover and by sleep, so a seventy year old with perfect habits is a very fit seventy year old rather than indistinguishable from a very fit twenty five year old. A night puts back less as you get older, which is the first thing about ageing anybody recognises. **The bug that mattered:** age was computed by dividing milliseconds by 365.25 days, which is a day short every fourth year — so one birthday in four reported the wrong number and the history read `Turned 61` twice with no `62`. It is counted by the calendar now, in one function, and `cinema.ts` and `going.ts` each had their own copy which now point at it.

    **The size you end up.** The player sets an adult height and weight at creation and a character who starts young grows to exactly them, on one curve. One, not two: boys and girls finish growing at different ages, an honest model of that needs a real growth reference and a field saying which to use, and SLATE has neither — so it carries the middle and says so rather than guessing from a pronoun. Only the player's character gets a size; the checker fails if anybody else is given one.

    **What is real.** Life expectancy at birth for 160 countries and the state pension age for 115, both published figures in whole years, plus the general national minimum age for driving, drinking, working, marrying, voting, signing, leasing and borrowing across the 41 countries that set something other than the world default, and the 10 where alcohol is sold to nobody at any age. Every one of those is labelled: the pension note says many countries still set two ages and SLATE carries the higher; the gate note says these are national minimums with exceptions and are not a statute.

    **The correction the gate caught.** The checker refused Nigeria and Zimbabwe for pensioning at sixty and sixty five while living to fifty four and sixty two — and the checker was right that something was wrong, but the data was not it. Life expectancy at birth is an average over everybody born, including the children who do not reach five, and where that is high it drags the whole figure a long way below the age adults actually reach. A game that used the at-birth figure to decide when people die would kill a Nigerian character at fifty four. There is an adult figure now, labelled as SLATE's own adjustment rather than a life table, and it is what the world uses to decide anything.

    **Context decides what is open to you.** The rule applied is the one where you are standing rather than the one on your passport: a nineteen year old is served in Berlin, refused in Los Angeles and refused in Tokyo, and the refusal names the country, the age it sets and how many years short you are. Where alcohol is not sold at all the room says that rather than quoting an age that does not exist. Ten commands ask before they act, and so does the door of a bar. Marriage, engagement and children were already built and still run end to end; the test walks it.

    **And the rule that shaped all of it: nothing may tell the player when they are going to die.** The generation above you gets an end drawn from a band around the adult expectancy, written onto the schedule and onto nothing a screen can reach — the calendar item is called A Quiet Day and names nobody. The player's own character is deliberately given no such date at all. A passing is reported with a name, an age and what they were to you, and never a cause the game invented. Four tests and a checker rule exist for nothing but this.

    **Where a name comes from, and who is who.** A family shares a name, and which name depends on where the family is from — so `content/heritage.ts` carries 54 heritages across 18 regions with 820 given names and 736 family names, drawn from published national and regional name lists. The player picks their own heritage and one for each of the two people who raised them, so a family of two traditions names its children out of both, and a button on the creation page suggests a name from whichever was chosen. A heritage is a naming tradition and nothing else: it carries no attribute, no skill, no advantage and no disadvantage, and both the checker and a test fail the build if a heritage ever grows a numeric field. Everybody in a family now has a gender the game knows, so the page says Mother, Father, Sister and Brother rather than listing four people called Parent.

    **Held by the gate.** `tools/lifespan-check.mjs` measures every published figure against the geography and against a range a country has actually recorded, the ageing curve for monotonicity and for cliffs, the growth curves, the gates against every country that sets one, a generated family for impossible ages, and the whole system for anything a character could not know; `tests/lifespan.test.ts` is 36 tests. Schema 61 adds `Person.adultHeightCm`, `Person.adultWeightKg`, `Person.heritageId`, `Person.gender`, `Person.parentHeritageIds`, and a load-time build of the family so a save from before this gets the one it always had. A browser tour picked a Yoruba mother and an Irish father, took a suggested name from each of three heritages, set an adult height of 191 cm at creation, found all of it on the character, and read the Your Life tab back: age, stage, size, what a year costs, the pension age, the four people born alongside, and eight gates each answered with the American rule.

48. Life contract item 9, second part — school. **"A full educational system catered to wherever im at in the world... play sports at school, get physical, have a full class schedule."**

    A save could not start younger than sixteen and there would have been nothing for it to do if it had. Now it can start at five.

    **The shape of an education, which is public record.** 31 systems covering 89 countries by name, and a general one everywhere else that says it is the general one. A fifteen year old is in Grade 10 in Los Angeles, Year 11 in London, Class 10 in Delhi, Seconde in Paris and the first year of high school in Tokyo, and what they sit at the end of it is a diploma, a GCSE, a Class 10 board examination, the Baccalauréat or the Gaokao. 94 stages and 46 named qualifications, plus the ages each country's law actually compels — **and the checker found two places that were wrong about that:** Sweden compels from six and grundskola started at seven, and Brazil compels from four and had nothing before six. Both have the missing year in them now.

    **A week that is a timetable.** Thirty lessons across thirteen subjects, built by quota and then dealt out under the constraint a real timetable is built under: nothing twice running and nothing more than twice in a day. Drawing each period at random had given a seven year old three periods of games in one day, which is not a timetable, it is a coincidence.

    **Marks that are earned.** Every subject starts where the child's aptitude for it is, and moves toward what they are capable of at the speed they are working — a term is a lot of days and nothing jumps. Skipping costs the mark and the attendance, and below four fifths the school rings home. What the average comes to says plainly whether a university would look, and whether it would pay.

    **Sport that is that country's sport.** 24 school sports, each listed only in the countries whose schools really field them: American football only in America, Australian rules only in Australia, cricket across the Commonwealth, kabaddi in South Asia, ice hockey in Canada and the Nordics. A trial is a trial and about half of them say no. Out of season is refused by name.

    **And the play, which is why any of this is in a game about film.** Twelve plays, all out of copyright and all genuinely staged by school drama departments — Macbeth, The Crucible, An Inspector Calls, A Doll's House. You are cast out of the drama club on what you can actually do on the day.

    **The design problem that had to be solved first.** The player's character is the sandbox one with every attribute at the ceiling, so a twelve year old walked into the audition with a hundred in everything and took the lead in Macbeth on day one. That is not a childhood. There is a youth curve now: what a character can bring is scaled by the years they have actually had, reaching everything they are in the early twenties. It caps nothing permanently and takes nothing off an adult — it is a ceiling on what has been delivered so far. The ladder it produces is the right one: ensemble at nine, a named part at thirteen, the lead at sixteen.

    **And the rule that makes being found as a child possible.** Being noticed is read against *raw* talent rather than what a young body can currently deliver, because somebody sitting at the back of a school hall is reading potential — which is exactly how a child actor is found. It still needs a real part, a good night, and a roll, and it comes off at about one school play in six for somebody genuinely gifted.

    **The honest limit, stated in the room.** There are millions of schools on earth and no feed to name them from, so every school in SLATE is SLATE's own — named for the district it stands in, or a saint only in the countries that really name schools after saints, because a St Martin High School in Tokyo is not a school anybody would believe. The first pass named a Beaconsfield Primary School in Riyadh and a St Martin's in Tokyo, and both are fixed.

    **Held by the gate.** `tools/school-check.mjs` measures every system for stage order, gaps, year naming and the compulsory years, every subject and club against a real attribute or skill, every sport against the countries that field it, a generated week in four countries for the timetable constraints, a school in ten cities named for where it stands, and the acting ladder for monotonicity; `tests/school.test.ts` is 26 tests. Schema 62 adds `universe.schooling`. A browser tour started a fourteen year old in Los Angeles, read the timetable and the marks off the page, worked a full day, joined the drama club and took a named part in The Crucible.

49. Life contract item 9, third part — university. **"Even go to college and get a major if my grades are good enough or if im on a sports or academic scholarship... real universities geographically."**

    Item 9 is complete with this. Unlike the schools, these are real: there are a few thousand universities in the world rather than a few million schools, and which town each one stands in is not in dispute.

    **197 real universities across 56 countries**, founded between 1088 and 2014, each with its real town and its real founding year — Oxford, Yale, the Sorbonne, Todai, Tsinghua, Bocconi, USP, Wits, UNAM — including the film and drama schools this game is actually about: RADA, LAMDA, Juilliard, the NFTS, La Fémis, VGIK, the Beijing Film Academy, NIDA, FAMU, the Łódź film school, the Korean Academy of Film Arts. 44 teach film and 21 teach drama.

    **The gazetteer problem, solved honestly.** SLATE carries 1,379 cities rather than every town, so Oxford, New Haven, Heidelberg and Beaconsfield are not in it. Rather than move a university to a city it is not in, each row carries its real town and is reached from the nearest carried city, and the page says exactly that: "Oxford, reached from London". A checker rule and a test both fail the build if a row ever says a university is in a city it is only reached from.

    **How a country pays for a degree is real, and it differs completely.** 57 regimes carried, 17 of them charging nothing at all: Norway nothing, Germany an administrative fee, England a capped home fee, Australia nothing now and a share of your earnings later, America whatever the university decides. The regime is public record; what lands on the invoice is SLATE's calibration in the local cost of living, and the note says which is which.

    **Admissions as a curve rather than a wall.** The first pass had a slope of two and a half points of chance per mark, which made everything below the very top effectively closed — somebody carrying seventy had a three per cent chance at University College London. Softened to a curve where matching what a place expects is a good chance rather than a certainty, so seventy-three now reads Oxford 19, RADA 31, UCL 33, Manchester 52. Below the bar is still a refusal, and it names the bar.

    **And a drama school auditions rather than reading marks**, which is the honest difference between applying to read law and standing up in a room, so a conservatoire will look at somebody the academic bar would refuse.

    **A place won on sport is not a universal arrangement.** It is American and Canadian (and, differently, Japanese and Korean); most of Europe has no such thing because sport there sits in clubs rather than universities. SLATE carries where it really happens, and both the checker and a test fail if an English university ever offers one.

    **The problem this part exposed in the last one.** School marks were being scaled by the youth curve built for part one, so the best possible sixteen year old could only reach sixty-four against a bar of sixty-eight — university was effectively unreachable for anybody who had gone through school. That curve is about what a young body and a young judgement can deliver in a room full of adults, and school is not that: a fifteen year old can and does top the year. School marks come off it now; the audition and the sports trial stay on it, which is where it belongs.

    **Two things the checker caught.** Juilliard was teaching economics, because the conservatoire rule keyed on having a single strength and Juilliard has two, music and drama — it keys on whether every strength is an arts field now. And University College London had no law faculty in the data, which is wrong about one of the better law schools in Britain.

    **Held by the gate.** `tools/university-check.mjs` measures every university against the geography and its own country, every founding year, every fee regime against the countries that have universities, the admissions curve for being a curve, the conservatoire rule, the majors against real attributes and skills, and the sport-place rule against western Europe; `tests/university.test.ts` is 23 tests. Schema 63 adds `universe.applications` and `universe.studies`. A browser tour started a seventeen year old in Los Angeles, read a list of real American universities with their real towns off the page, picked UCLA and film production, and got an honest answer: about 28 in a hundred.

SLATE: Complete Player-Facing Language And Tutorial Overhaul

Overhaul the writing throughout the entire current game so it reads naturally, clearly, and professionally.

The current language often sounds like internal engine documentation. It makes ordinary actions difficult to understand and breaks immersion. I want the clarity of a polished commercial game: understandable controls, natural feedback, useful notifications, and tutorials that actually teach me how to play.

This is a complete sweep of the retained game, not a sample rewrite or a few wording fixes.

1. Start From The Current Verified Build

Read the repository instructions and latest handoff. Confirm the actual branch, merge status, and local changes.

If the previous verification gate is still running, let it finish without modifying its checkout. Record that result before beginning this pass, or use an appropriately isolated checkout.

Preserve gameplay rules, calculations, saves, and existing work. Do not restore removed systems or change decision #4’s protected functionality or statuses.

This instruction authorizes replacing existing editorial conventions that cause unnatural player-facing language. Record those changes in the writing guidelines. Preserve my Title Case requirements for headings, navigation labels, and buttons; use natural sentence capitalization for paragraphs, explanations, and dialogue.

2. Inventory Every Source Of Player-Facing Text

Create a coverage checklist from the actual repository.

Include:

* Startup, save selection, character creation, and onboarding.
* Home and its retained actions.
* Player Profile, attributes, genres, mastery, and progression.
* School, classes, coursework, rehearsals, productions, assessments, and school completion.
* Money, banking, payments, fees, holds, statements, and transaction explanations.
* Papers, documents, applications, taxes, and status notices.
* Health, treatment, coverage, stamina, energy, and recovery.
* Calendar, schedules, time advancement, and reminders.
* Tutorials, guides, help panels, tooltips, and connected-system explanations.
* Notifications, invitations, feedback, and generated messages.
* Buttons, menus, tabs, filters, placeholders, empty states, loading states, and confirmations.
* Validation errors, unavailable-action reasons, failures, recovery instructions, and save/load messages.
* Accessibility labels and text used by assistive technology.

Search both interface components and engine-generated strings. Include shared templates, dynamically assembled sentences, pluralization, dates, amounts, and conditional variants.

Track every discovered source as:

* Rewritten And Verified.
* Reviewed And Retained, with a reason.
* Pending.
* Blocked, with a specific explanation.

Do not mark an entire feature complete because its landing page was rewritten. Verify its deeper interactions and alternate states.

3. Establish One Natural Writing Standard

Use this voice throughout SLATE:

* Direct, familiar English.
* Short, complete sentences where practical.
* Clear subjects and actions.
* Natural contractions where appropriate.
* Specific explanations grounded in the current situation.
* Consistent names for the same concepts.
* Professional language without forced slang.
* No unnecessary technical terminology or elaborate phrasing.

Avoid exposing internal concepts such as:

* “What the engine reads.”
* “The work’s inputs.”
* “The part most often short.”
* “The standing falls a step.”
* “A choice rehearsed in.”

Translate the meaning into language a player understands.

Examples of direction, not mandatory replacements:

* “Which Work Reads It” → “Where This Skill Matters.”
* “The standing falls a step” → name the actual academic consequence.
* “A choice rehearsed in” → explain how prepared the actor is to perform that interpretation.
* “An action was refused” → explain why the player cannot do it and what they can do next.

Do not blindly replace terms. Read the underlying mechanic and preserve its exact meaning.

Keep internal code identifiers stable unless changing them is genuinely necessary. Player-facing labels can improve without renaming saved fields or commands.

4. Give Different Types Of Text Appropriate Voices

Navigation And Buttons:

* Use familiar, concise labels.
* Make the action clear.
* Avoid ambiguous verbs or clever wording that obscures the destination.

Notifications:

* Lead with what happened.
* Explain the practical consequence.
* Give a relevant next action when one exists.

Faculty Feedback:

* Sound like a teacher responding to this specific work.
* Identify the observed problem or strength.
* Explain a useful next step.
* Do not invent dialogue, observations, or personal knowledge the simulation does not support.
* Preserve appropriate labeling of simulated institutional feedback.

Banking And Official Notices:

* Use clear professional language.
* Preserve exact amounts, dates, deadlines, conditions, and uncertainty.
* Separate verified facts from simulation assumptions.
* Do not make legal or financial consequences sound more certain than the implementation supports.

Narration:

* Describe the actual event naturally.
* Reflect relevant history and current state.
* Avoid melodrama, repetitive filler, or invented events.

Errors And Unavailable Actions:

* Explain the specific obstacle.
* State whether anything was charged, changed, or saved when relevant.
* Provide the next legitimate step.
* Never promise recovery or eligibility the game does not offer.

5. Rebuild Tutorials Around Actual Player Tasks

Every feature’s tutorial should answer:

1. What is this for?
2. Where do I go?
3. What do I select?
4. What do I need first?
5. What happens when I do it?
6. How does it affect other parts of my game?
7. What should I do next?
8. What if I cannot do it?

Use progressive detail:

* A short plain-language overview.
* Concrete steps using the real interface labels.
* Expected results and consequences.
* Optional deeper explanations and calculations.

Verify every navigation path and instruction against the built application. Do not write instructions based on guessed controls or an idealized future interface.

Explain distinctions that can otherwise confuse players, including:

* Health, stamina, and current energy.
* Attributes, techniques, genre knowledge, and practice.
* Current readiness versus a fully prepared forecast.
* Choosing an interpretation versus rehearsing it.
* Available funds versus pending funds.
* Applying for a document versus receiving it.
* A warning versus the later formal evaluation.

Keep tutorials contextual. Show the relevant explanation for the player’s actual stage and state.

Link to connected features where helpful. Following a tutorial link and returning should preserve context and must not accidentally perform an action or advance time.

6. Preserve Mechanical Accuracy And Depth

Do not simplify text by removing important conditions.

For every consequential message, verify:

* The reason matches the actual calculation or validation.
* The amount, date, duration, and status are correct.
* The next action is available or its prerequisites are stated.
* The explanation distinguishes estimates from recorded outcomes.
* The wording does not promise guaranteed success where none exists.
* The text agrees with connected screens and tutorials.

Keep precise details available through drill-downs. The initial explanation should be readable without requiring the player to understand formulas.

Use shared engine values and existing explanation helpers where appropriate. Avoid copying numerical rules into prose that will become stale.

Do not alter gameplay to make a sentence easier to write. If the sweep reveals a real functional defect, record it separately, fix it where authorized and necessary, and test the behavior.

7. Make Dynamic Text Read Correctly

Exercise generated messages across their meaningful variants:

* Singular and plural.
* Zero, one, and many items.
* Different names and pronouns.
* Missing optional information.
* Different academic standings.
* Success, partial progress, failure, and recovery.
* Deadlines that are upcoming, due, or missed.
* Positive, zero, and negative balances where applicable.
* New players, advanced players, and completed school journeys.

Prevent sentence fragments, repeated punctuation, awkward concatenation, raw identifiers, malformed dates, and inconsistent terminology.

Do not sacrifice important information simply to shorten text.

8. Handle Repetition Through Context

Rewrite repetitive message patterns so they acknowledge:

* What has changed.
* What remains unresolved.
* What the player has already done.
* Why the recommendation or restriction still applies.
* The next useful action.

Use variation where it remains accurate, but do not randomly paraphrase identical messages to pretend the simulation changed.

Preserve continuity. A teacher should not describe an unchanged weakness as a brand-new discovery every time.

For each batch:

Check desktop and mobile for wrapping, clipping, overflowing buttons, excessive text walls, and inaccessible explanations.

Run the required gate and production build after integration. Verify that the writing changes have not broken tutorials, routes, command matching, accessibility, saves, or gameplay.

Automated checks cannot prove that writing feels natural. Perform an editorial review as well, and provide a manual review route for me.

Update the handoff and repository guidance so future agency and career features follow this writing standard automatically.

Commit and push the completed work and prepare the appropriate PR. Follow the established merge and deployment authorization rules.

Do not call the sweep complete while identified player-facing text remains unreviewed. Do not claim human usability approval from automated tests.

The intended result is SLATE speaking naturally to the player: clear controls, understandable consequences, believable feedback, and tutorials that make the game easy to learn without losing its depth.

Read the repository instructions, latest handoff, writing guidelines, glossary, text inventory, and previous language reports. Verify the actual current branch and source state.

3. Apply This Writing Standard

SLATE should speak to the player like a finished game.

Use:

* Familiar words.
* Direct instructions.
* Clear subjects and verbs.
* Natural contractions where appropriate.
* Specific consequences.
* Concise explanations with optional deeper detail.
* Consistent terminology.
* A voice appropriate to the speaker and situation.

Avoid:

* Engine terminology exposed as ordinary narration.
* Awkward passive constructions.
* Cryptic fragments.
* Overly literary explanations of basic controls.
* Repeated references to “this life,” “the work reads,” or “the game judges” where ordinary language would be clearer.
* Forced slang, exaggerated enthusiasm, or unnecessary filler.
* Replacing one obscure phrase with another.

Examples of the intended direction:

“A word stays plain text until the chapter that opens it has been read.”
→ “Some links are locked until you complete the related tutorial.”

“The school’s commitments aren’t yours to skip.”
→ “School events don’t have a Skip option.”

“This life was begun without the guide.”
→ “You started without the tutorial.”

“Checking is short.”
→ “You don’t have enough available money in your checking account.”

These examples establish the voice. Before using them, verify that each preserves the actual behavior and established terminology.

Do not merely replace a blacklist of words. Read the entire sentence and the interaction around it.

5. Make Tutorials Easy To Follow

Teach tasks in the order the player performs them.

Each relevant tutorial should explain:

* What the feature is for.
* Where to find it.
* Which actual control to select.
* What is required first.
* What happens next.
* What the result means.
* What connects to it.
* What to do if the action is unavailable.

Start with a short explanation and actionable steps. Put formulas, exceptions, and advanced conditions behind optional details.

Do not bury the first action beneath several paragraphs of background.

Use exact interface labels. If a label is renamed, update every tutorial, reference, accessibility label, and relevant test consistently.

Distinguish clearly between:

* Choosing an interpretation and rehearsing it.
* Current readiness and fully prepared potential.
* Attributes, techniques, and genre knowledge.
* Health, stamina, and current energy.
* Pending and available money.
* Applying for a document and receiving it.
* A warning and a formal consequence.

Verify instructions by following them in the built game. Finding a label in code is only partial evidence; identify any route not exercised.

6. Give Each Kind Of Text Its Own Appropriate Voice

Instructor Feedback:

* Sound like a teacher responding to the actual performance.
* Name a specific strength or weakness.
* Give an actionable next step where appropriate.
* Do not invent observations, dialogue, or knowledge unsupported by the simulation.

Tutorials:

* Explain clearly and patiently.
* Avoid assuming the player understands engine terminology.
* Use examples that match actual mechanics.

Banking And Official Notices:

* Be straightforward and professional.
* Preserve amounts, dates, requirements, and conditions.
* Label estimates and simulation assumptions accurately.

Notifications:

* Lead with what happened.
* Explain the consequence.
* Offer a relevant next action.

Buttons:

* Name the action clearly.
* Keep labels concise and distinguishable.
* Do not rely on surrounding prose to explain an ambiguous button.

Errors:

* Explain what failed.
* Accurately state whether anything changed or was charged.
* Give a legitimate recovery step.
* Keep technical diagnostics available separately.

7. Review Complete Interactions

Do not approve sentences only in isolation.

Read the sequence from entry to completion:

* The feature description.
* The selected action.
* Any prerequisite or refusal.
* The confirmation.
* The immediate result.
* The later notification.
* The relevant tutorial and drill-down.

Remove redundant explanations, inconsistent terminology, abrupt voice changes, and missing next steps.

Verify that the player receives important information at the moment it is useful.

8. Make Repetition Context-Aware

Repeated messages must acknowledge relevant history:

* What the player already attempted.
* What improved.
* What remains unresolved.
* Why the recommendation still applies.
* What action is useful now.

Do not repeat a weakness as if it were discovered for the first time.

Do not invent new circumstances for variety or rotate to worse advice. Stable labels and recurring requirements should remain consistent.

Review dynamic messages across meaningful variants, including singular/plural, missing optional information, names, pronouns, dates, amounts, deadlines, and changed statuses.

9. Preserve Meaning And Mechanics

This is primarily an editorial pass.

Do not alter calculations, progression, eligibility, deadlines, costs, or outcomes to make wording easier.

For consequential text, verify its meaning against the implementation. Preserve necessary qualifications without making the opening sentence unreadable.

Keep code identifiers and saved fields stable where possible. Rename display labels independently.

If the review reveals a functional defect, record it separately. Fix necessary in-scope defects with regression tests rather than hiding them through different wording.

Do not silently rewrite authentic quotations as if the new words were the original source. Clearly distinguish quoted material from SLATE’s explanations.

10. Use A Real Editorial Acceptance Standard

For each source, assess:

* Naturalness.
* Immediate clarity.
* Mechanical accuracy.
* Consistency with the glossary.
* Fit for its speaker and context.
* Usefulness of its next-step guidance.
* Readability in the actual interface.

Do not award a self-declared “10/10” or treat a green automated check as proof of good prose.

A retained source must pass these same standards. “Already reviewed,” “grammatically correct,” and “contains no flagged words” are insufficient reasons.

Inspect all eight major areas again after integration to catch different voices introduced by separate batches.

SLATE — Contextual Writing, Distinct Voices, And Clear Information

Implement this work in the existing game. This is an implementation request, not a request for another proposal or audit alone.

GOAL

Make SLATE’s player-facing writing natural, understandable, specific to what actually happened, and varied during extended play.

The current writing is improved, but recurring school feedback repeats fixed openings, teachers sound interchangeable, and practice recommendations overwhelm the player with calculations.

Fix those underlying problems while preserving the existing simulation, mechanics, saves, and approved scope.

1. START FROM THE ACTUAL CURRENT BUILD

Read the repository instructions, current handoff, latest main, and relevant open changes before editing. Preserve existing work.

Verify the issues against current code and rendered screens. Do not assume an old review still describes every file.

Keep the previously deferred decision #4 items deferred. Do not restore removed personal-life, travel, shopping, housing, or industry systems.

2. BUILD FEEDBACK FROM WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED

Create or improve the shared feedback system so its messages can use relevant existing facts:

- The assignment, scene, role, course, and genre.

- What the player prepared and left unfinished.

- The approach or interpretation chosen.

- The strongest contribution and most important limitation.

- The partner’s actual contribution.

- Relevant fatigue, stress, injury, or other conditions.

- Previous feedback on the same issue.

- Whether the player improved, resolved it, maintained progress, or slipped back.

- One practical next action available in the current game.

Choose the facts that matter for this particular message. Do not include every fact in every response.

A feedback message should usually communicate:

1. What happened.

2. Why it happened.

3. What to try next, when a next step is useful.

Every claim must be grounded in actual game state or evaluation data. Do not invent observed acting choices, dialogue, feelings, events, or causes that the simulation does not record.

If the engine only knows a numerical result, explain that result honestly instead of pretending it observed a specific performance moment.

3. REMOVE REPETITIVE FEEDBACK STRUCTURES

Inspect feedbackWords, ADVANCED, teacher term reports, director notes, partner responses, practice advice, and equivalent systems in the current code.

Fix patterns such as every successful assignment beginning:

“It came together. Here’s what to try next.”

Changing a few synonyms is not sufficient.

Vary the structure according to the situation:

- A newly achieved improvement.

- A strength maintained over several pieces.

- A recurring weakness.

- Partial progress.

- A setback after earlier improvement.

- A strong result despite a recorded difficulty.

- Preparation that improved but was held back by another factor.

- An unchanged result where no new advice is justified.

Use only supported situations.

Sometimes a short acknowledgment is enough. Do not generate a new lecture after every routine action.

Repeated advice can remain necessary when the same problem persists. Acknowledge that continuity and explain what remains unresolved instead of presenting the same finding as new.

4. ADD MEMORY FOR PRESENTATION

Track recent narrative wording at appropriate levels, such as the speaker, event type, and player save.

Avoid repeating the same opening or complete message within a recent-history window when another equally accurate option exists.

Keep this memory bounded and compatible with saves and reloads.

Save/reload should not reroll feedback already delivered. Text selection must not consume gameplay randomness or alter simulation outcomes.

When all suitable variants have been used, prefer an accurate repetition over an inaccurate new claim. Never sacrifice truth for novelty.

Keep button names, numerical labels, warnings, and recurring instructions stable where consistency helps the player.

5. GIVE SPEAKERS DISTINCT, APPROPRIATE VOICES

Differentiate feedback by teaching role and context:

- Acting feedback emphasizes objectives, relationships, listening, and choices.

- Voice feedback emphasizes breath, vocal expression, clarity, and sustained delivery where the engine supports those observations.

- Movement feedback emphasizes physical expression, coordination, and stamina where supported.

- Text-analysis feedback emphasizes interpretation and textual understanding.

- Directors address the production and the player’s recorded work.

- Classmates respond according to established relationships and events.

Use fictionalized game voices. Do not claim to reproduce a real teacher’s actual personality or speech.

Keep a clear, unobtrusive indication that simulated feedback is written by SLATE rather than quoted from real people.

Voices should differ in focus and phrasing without becoming caricatures, exaggerated accents, or catchphrases.

6. MAKE THE PROFILE EASY TO READ

Replace the wall of practice forecasts with a concise primary recommendation.

Show first:

- What to work on.

- Why it matters now.

- The action to take.

- The main expected benefit, clearly identified as an estimate.

- Any immediate reason the action is unavailable.

Put supporting calculations behind a clearly labeled expandable section, such as “Why This Recommendation” or “View Forecast Details.”

Keep available:

- The forecast’s assumptions.

- Relevant recent work.

- Preparation versus training comparisons.

- Discipline’s contribution.

- Alternative recommendations.

- Expected attribute and performance changes.

- Uncertainty and limitations.

Do not repeat the same assumptions in every recommendation. Present shared explanations once.

Use the actual current values. Do not hard-code sample numbers.

7. APPLY THE SAME CLARITY TO BANKING, PAPERS, AND SCHOOL

For lengthy explanations, use this order:

- What this means for the player now.

- What they can do.

- What it costs, changes, or requires.

- Optional full rules and sources.

Keep consequential fees, deadlines, eligibility requirements, irreversible outcomes, and warnings visible before the player confirms an action.

Do not hide essential information behind an optional explanation.

Preserve precise amounts and conditions. Where wording and gameplay disagree, trace the implementation before rewriting. Do not conceal a behavioral defect by making the text vague.

Keep any unrelated banking or legal-rule changes separately identified rather than silently changing mechanics during this writing work.

8. FINISH THE TUTORIAL’S CLARITY

Review the tutorial and contextual help in the actual interface.

Every instruction must:

- Use the exact visible control name.

- Explain the immediate result.

- Explain relevant automatic behavior.

- State prerequisites clearly.

- Provide a usable next step when blocked.

- Link to connected help where useful.

Replace vague references such as “it happens” when the event is unclear.

Break up paragraphs that contain several different rules.

Preserve established Title Case rules for headings and controls, with ordinary sentence case in prose.

If a control’s wording itself is misleading, rename it consistently across the interface, tutorials, accessibility labels, and tests. Preserve internal identifiers and save compatibility where possible.

9. PRESERVE THE STRONG CREATIVE WRITING

Keep the specificity and distinct situations in the original acting briefs.

Do not flatten vivid, clear scene writing into generic instructions.

Apply concise language to controls and explanations, while allowing dramatic material to have atmosphere and character.

Do not replace meaningful genre differences with cosmetic wording changes.

10. VERIFY BOTH WRITING AND FUNCTION

Before editing, record a reproducible baseline of:

- Repeated feedback openings.

- Exact duplicate narrative messages.

- Teacher feedback shared across courses.

- Dense recommendation blocks.

- Tutorial instructions that are difficult to follow.

After implementation, compare equivalent scenarios on the same underlying gameplay state.

Run multiple meaningful playthroughs covering:

- A new player learning the controls.

- An attentive student who prepares consistently.

- An underprepared student with recurring notes.

- A student who improves after feedback.

- A student who resolves an issue and later regresses.

- A highly skilled student maintaining strong work.

- A student affected by fatigue or injury.

- Repeated work with a partner.

- Different genres and performance approaches.

- Banking and papers tasks involving deadlines or blocked actions.

- Save/reload during these journeys.

Reuse suitable existing fixtures and automation. Avoid rerunning identical scenarios simply to inflate the test count.

Read the resulting player-facing transcripts in sequence. Automated string checks alone cannot establish naturalness.

Check desktop and mobile for:

- Readable summaries.

- Working expandable details.

- Clear warnings.

- Correct links and controls.

- Keyboard and screen-reader access.

- No overflow or broken layouts.

Verify that:

- Text agrees with the actual outcome.

- Feedback history survives reloads.

- Narrative variation does not change gameplay results.

- Necessary warnings remain visible.

- Shared labels remain consistent.

- Repeated feedback acknowledges previous events accurately.

Fix defects introduced or exposed within this work’s scope, and rerun the affected checks. Run the required full regression gate once the changes are ready.

11. PROVIDE REVIEWABLE PROOF

Deliver:

- The exact tested commit.

- A concise list of implemented changes.

- Before/after examples from actual rendered gameplay.

- Consecutive feedback transcripts showing improvement, repetition, and recovery over time.

- Examples demonstrating distinct teaching voices.

- Profile recommendations before and after.

- Measured repetition results, with the measurement method and exclusions.

- Test commands, results, failures, and fixes.

- Any remaining limitations or unverified behavior.

Distinguish source-text review, automated tests, browser checks, and human editorial judgment.

Do not claim “10/10,” “zero bugs,” or “never repetitive” merely because automated tests pass.

COMPLETION

Continue through implementation, editorial review, relevant playthroughs, debugging, and the repository’s required verification gates. Do not stop after creating a framework, adding a few examples, or writing a completion document.

Finish the agreed scope with evidence. If an external blocker prevents completion, identify the exact blocker and preserve a concrete handoff.

The desired result is clear instructions, concise summaries, truthful contextual feedback, recognizable speaker differences, and sustained variety grounded in the player’s actual journey.

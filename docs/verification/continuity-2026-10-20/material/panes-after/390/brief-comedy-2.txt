The Best Man’s Draft
Out The Door By Seven

On his first night out since the twins were born, a father briefing the teenage babysitter can’t stop adding instructions, while his wife waits outside with the engine running.

THE SCENE
Where And When
The kitchen of a small house at five to seven on a Friday, the eight-month-old twins asleep upstairs. Their anniversary table across town was booked six weeks ago for half past seven. He has made a color-coded schedule, laminated it and stuck it to the fridge. His wife went out to wait in the car ten minutes ago.
The Two Of You
Neighbors. She has looked after half the children on the street; he has never left the twins with anyone but his mother.
You Play
Gideon Achterberg, thirty-six, a dentist, in a jacket he hasn’t worn in a year, with the baby monitor in his hand.
The Reader Plays
Tess, sixteen, the neighbor’s daughter, eldest of five, calm, and different with every instruction: bored, alarmed, amused.
How They Play It
Unpredictable: playing opposite it, the tape counts responsiveness and consistency for more and leans on your adaptability and composure.
The Genre Asks
Comic Rhythm: Setup and payoff: cues picked up, pauses placed, the laugh landing where the timing puts it. A self-tape in it is judged more on responsiveness and execution, and calls on your adaptability and focus. Rehearsing on your feet: timing lives in the exchange and is found by playing it through.
Comic timing, the pauses, cadence and pacing that land a joke, is named the core comic skill. This comes from the genre’s research, mostly held with low confidence.
What You Want
Leave the house by seven, sure the twins will be fine without him.
What’s In The Way
Every instruction reminds him of two more, she seems far too relaxed about all of them, and each “last thing” costs a minute his wife is counting.
WHERE IT TURNS
He walks her through the schedule on the fridge at speed, and she nods at every line. (Comic)
The car horn sounds; he says “Last thing,” and shows her how to test a bottle on the inside of a wrist, on his own. (Comic)
She asks one quiet question, whether he has ever left them before, and he admits he hasn’t and that he’s terrified. (Grave and Comic, held at once)
She tells him what she’ll do if one of them wakes, and it’s better than anything on his schedule; he’s relieved and a little hurt. (Comic and Lyric, held at once)
He makes it to the door, comes back to tell her the twins’ names in case she forgets them, and goes, leaving the monitor on the counter. (Comic)

3 changes of tone between beats and 2 beats held two ways. Rehearsal is complete at 50% in a beat, up to 15% more in a beat a change leads into, and 8% more in one held two ways.

WHAT OUT THE DOOR BY SEVEN ASKS
Everyday Speech, on camera
Timing: Built On It. He reads the schedule at speed, in a rhythm the horn cuts across, and every “last thing” has to come just after he seemed to be done.
Physical Work: Much. He puts his jacket on and takes it off again, tests a bottle on his own wrist and starts for the door twice. Play the business on your side of the frame, with the schedule, the bottle and the monitor as props and the door off frame.
Playing Off The Partner: Much. Each new instruction answers how she took the last one, and she never takes two the same way.
Freedom To Improvise: Much. The schedule is written. Each “last thing” after it is yours to find as you tape, and the reader’s mood changes with each one.
Tone: comic to grave to lyric, 3 changes of tone to play. A briefing at speed, a demonstration she didn’t need, a confession, a teenager who knows more than he does, and an exit that takes two tries.
Techniques It Asks: Comedy, Improvisation. The laughs are in where each new instruction lands against the horn and her calm, so the scene asks for comic timing, and the last things you find as you tape ask for improvisation.

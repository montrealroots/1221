The Boxes In The Hall
A practice brief for Domestic Drama: an original scene written for SLATE, taped to a reader off camera.
The Thermostat
The Boxes In The Hall

A mother who has just begun to enjoy her empty house comes home to find her twenty-year-old son has left college and moved back in without asking.

THE SCENE
Where And When
The kitchen of a small house on a Sunday evening in October. Her son left for college two years ago; since then she has turned his room into a sewing room and started a pottery class on Sundays. She came home from it to find his boxes stacked in the hall and him at the kitchen table, eating cereal.
The Two Of You
Mother and only son, close and teasing, who have never once talked about how lonely the house was when it was just the two of them.
You Play
Annette Osei, fifty, a dental hygienist, still in her coat, with clay under her nails.
The Reader Plays
Elliot, twenty, her son, who jokes and goes silent by turns.
How They Play It
Unpredictable: playing opposite it, the tape counts responsiveness and consistency for more and leans on your adaptability and composure.
The Genre Asks
Played Between Two: The scene lives between the two of you: chemistry, contrast, a part each that only works against the other. A self-tape in it is judged more on connection and responsiveness, and calls on your presence, listening and responsiveness and emotional depth. Rehearsing on your feet: a partnership is built in the exchange, played through until each part answers the other.
Played Straight: Meant completely: the feeling played with full conviction, never winked at, however large or strange the material. A self-tape in it is judged more on believability and connection, and calls on your emotional depth and presence. The breakdown and the lines: believing it starts with knowing exactly what your character wants and why it matters to them.
Descended from the realist stage, which asked for psychologically real acting and ensemble playing. This comes from the genre’s research, mostly held with low confidence.
What You Want
Tell him he can’t move back in, without him hearing that she doesn’t want him.
What’s In The Way
He’s frightened under the jokes, she wants him there as much as she doesn’t, and he has already unpacked the kettle.
WHERE IT TURNS
She sees the boxes, and they joke about the cereal, the sewing room and the pottery. (Comic)
He says he’s left college; she asks why, and he won’t say. (Grave)
She tells him about the pottery class, the quiet, the evenings that are hers now, and hears how it sounds. (Comic and Grave, held at once)
He tells her why he left, and it’s worse than she thought. (Grave)
She takes off her coat and says he can stay until spring, on conditions, and they both pretend it’s a compromise. (Lyric and Comic, held at once)

4 changes of tone between beats and 2 beats held two ways. Rehearsal is complete at 50% in a beat, up to 15% more in a beat a change leads into, and 8% more in one held two ways.

WHAT THE BOXES IN THE HALL ASKS
Everyday Speech, on camera
Timing: Some
Physical Work: Some. She keeps her coat on through most of the scene, shifting a box on her side of the frame, and taking the coat off is the turn.
Playing Off The Partner: Built On It. He swings between jokes and silence, and she can’t plan a line: each one comes from which son is at the table.
Tone: comic to grave to lyric, 4 changes of tone to play. Jokes at the door, a reason he won’t give, her own life said aloud, his reason, then a compromise they both see through.

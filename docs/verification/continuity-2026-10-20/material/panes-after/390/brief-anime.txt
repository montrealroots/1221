Last Launch From The Roof
The Spillway Walk

Dubbing an existing scene, an apprentice glider pilot tells her rival on the academy roof that she’s leaving tomorrow, and the lines have to fit mouths already drawn.

THE SCENE
Where And When
A dubbing booth. On the screen, a flight academy’s rooftop at sunset, gliders tied down in a rising wind; the animation is finished and every line must land on the lip movements already in it. In the story, she has just failed her final flight and has been sent home.
The Two Of You
Rivals since their first day, who have never once admitted they’re friends.
You Play
Haru, an apprentice pilot of seventeen, who laughs when she’s about to cry.
You Speak To
Rin, her rival, already recorded on the track. Play against his lines in your headphones.
The Genre Asks
Played Large: Feeling worn on the outside and sized to fill the frame: the voice and the body carry it as much as the face. A self-tape in it is judged more on execution and connection, and calls on your vocal expression, physical expression and presence. Rehearsing on your feet: a size the body and the voice can carry has to be found aloud and standing.
Dense Text, Exact: A lot of words, or difficult ones, delivered fluently and at speed: jargon, argument or deduction as second nature. A self-tape in it is judged more on execution and interpretation, and calls on your memory and focus. Learning the lines: fluency at speed is the lines learned past knowing them.
Dubbing asks for emotional truth sized to the drawn image while the timing fits mouth movements that already exist. This comes from the genre’s research, mostly held with low confidence.
What You Want
Say goodbye without letting him see it matters.
What’s In The Way
He won’t accept it, and every line has to fit a mouth that opens and closes on the picture’s timing, not hers.
WHERE IT TURNS
She jokes about the failed landing, fitting each quip to the drawn laugh. (Comic)
He says she’s the best pilot in the year, and her answer comes out too fast for the drawn mouth, so she has to find it again. (Comic and Grave, held at once)
She admits she wanted to fly against him forever, as the drawn sun goes down. (Lyric)

2 changes of tone between beats and 1 beat held two ways. Rehearsal is complete at 50% in a beat, up to 15% more in a beat a change leads into, and 8% more in one held two ways.

WHAT LAST LAUNCH FROM THE ROOF ASKS
Everyday Speech
Timing: Built On It. Every line fits mouth movements that already exist, so where each word falls is fixed by the picture.
Playing Off The Partner: Some
Vocal Demand: Much. The laugh that turns to tears is carried by the voice alone, and the volume rises with the wind on the track.
Tone: comic to grave to lyric, 2 changes of tone to play. Bravado, a crack she has to fit back into the picture, then the plain admission.
Techniques It Asks: Voice-Over. It’s a dub: you record alone at a microphone, in sync with the finished animation and his recorded lines.
It Only Takes: Set. The animation is finished, so every line has to land where the drawn lips already move. It can only be played set, the same way every time.

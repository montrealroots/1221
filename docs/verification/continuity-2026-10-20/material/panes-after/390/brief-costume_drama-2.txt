Quarter Day
The Second Fitting

At a bride’s last fitting in 1878, a dressmaker finds the bodice won’t close by an inch, and has to let the girl know she has guessed why, and will keep it, without a word that could be repeated.

THE SCENE
Where And When
A dressmaker’s shop in a market town, a wet afternoon in October 1878, three days before the wedding. The bride has just tried on the bodice behind a screen and handed it back out over the top: it won’t fasten by an inch, though it fitted a month ago. She’s changing back into her day dress now, out of sight, and the bride’s mother is coming at four to see the gown finished.
The Two Of You
Tradeswoman and customer, though the dressmaker has made the girl’s every dress since her christening robe, and the girl calls her by her first name when her mother isn’t there.
You Play
Mrs. Martha Lamb, forty-five, dressmaker and widow, with a pincushion at her wrist and the bodice in her hands.
The Reader Plays
Miss Clara Whitcombe, nineteen, out of sight behind the screen, talking brightly about everything but the bodice.
How They Play It
Steady: playing opposite it asks nothing more than the scene does.
The Genre Asks
Another Age’s Manners: How people of another time stood, moved, spoke and kept their place, kept up without looking like effort. A self-tape in it is judged more on execution and interpretation, and calls on your physical expression, discipline and vocal expression. The breakdown and rehearsal together: the manners are researched first, then made habit on your feet.
Actors learn period movement and behavior, deportment, greetings and bows, and sometimes period speech. This comes from the genre’s research, mostly held with low confidence.
What You Want
Let Clara know she has guessed, and that the gown will keep the secret, without saying a word that could be repeated.
What’s In The Way
A dressmaker known to have hidden such a thing would lose every customer in the county, the girl’s mother pays the bills and will inspect every seam at four, and Clara is too frightened to say anything but how happy she is.
WHERE IT TURNS
Martha makes pleasantries over the bodice while Clara chatters from behind the screen about the wedding breakfast. (Comic)
Martha says, as if in passing, that brides often change a little in the last month, and Clara goes quiet behind the screen. (Grave)
Martha announces that a fuller cut is the fashion in town this season, a reason so poor that Clara laughs at it. (Comic and Grave, held at once)
Clara comes to the edge of the screen and asks what Martha means to tell her mother, and Martha answers with one plain sentence. (Grave)

3 changes of tone between beats and 1 beat held two ways. Rehearsal is complete at 50% in a beat, up to 15% more in a beat a change leads into, and 8% more in one held two ways.

WHAT THE SECOND FITTING ASKS
Heightened Prose, on camera: The lines keep the manners of 1878 between a tradeswoman and her better, on a subject neither of them would name aloud: she may hint and apologize, and the plain sentence is kept for the end.
Timing: Some
Physical Work: Some. She unpicks the bodice’s side seams with a small seam ripper, at a worktable on her side of the frame, in a period dress and a fitted bodice of her own. The work in her hands is how she avoids looking up.
Playing Off The Partner: Much. Clara is out of sight behind the screen, so Martha has to read her from her voice alone and choose each hint by how the last one landed.
Tone: comic to grave, 3 changes of tone to play. Happy chatter, a hint that lands, an excuse so thin it’s funny, then the promise in a sentence.

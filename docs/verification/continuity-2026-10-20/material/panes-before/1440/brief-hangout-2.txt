Sunday At The Laundromat
No Meteors Tonight

Two neighbors who only know each other from the roof of their building wait in lawn chairs for a meteor shower behind the clouds, and neither wants to be first to go down.

THE SCENE
Where And When
The flat roof of a six-story apartment building on a warm August night, after midnight. The meteor shower was supposed to peak at one; the clouds came in at eleven. They met up here three summers ago and have never once run into each other anywhere else in the building. For the tape, lean back in a lawn chair, looking up past the lens: the sky is wherever she says it is.
The Two Of You
Roof friends: they know each other’s opinions on everything and not each other’s last names.
You Play
June Tremaine, forty-four, a night-shift radiographer on her night off.
The Reader Plays
Walt, sixty, from the fourth floor, who brought the thermos and keeps announcing he’s going to bed.
How They Play It
Unpredictable: playing opposite it, the tape counts responsiveness and consistency for more and leans on your adaptability and composure.
The Genre Asks
Played Between Two: The scene lives between the two of you: chemistry, contrast, a part each that only works against the other. A self-tape in it is judged more on connection and responsiveness, and calls on your presence, listening and responsiveness and emotional depth. Rehearsing on your feet: a partnership is built in the exchange, played through until each part answers the other.
Caught Live: Behavior that looks unrehearsed: real listening, the next line taken from what the other person just did. A self-tape in it is judged more on connection and responsiveness, and calls on your listening and responsiveness and adaptability. Rehearsing on your feet: what looks unrehearsed is found by playing it through until nothing is set but the wants.
Unhurried time with likeable characters: the pleasure is their interaction, not the plot. This comes from the genre’s research, mostly held with low confidence.
What You Want
Keep Walt on the roof until the clouds clear.
What’s In The Way
He has an early start, says so every ten minutes, and then thinks of one more thing to say.
WHERE IT TURNS
They rate the clouds out of ten and argue about the scoring. (Comic)
Walt goes off on his late wife’s theory of stars, and June asks the right questions to keep him going. (Comic and Lyric, held at once)
A quiet stretch, and she admits the roof is her favorite part of the week. (Lyric)
A streak, or maybe a plane, and they argue about whether it counts until he forgets he was leaving. (Comic)

3 changes of tone between beats and 1 beat held two ways. Rehearsal is complete at 50% in a beat, up to 15% more in a beat a change leads into, and 8% more in one held two ways.

WHAT NO METEORS TONIGHT ASKS
Everyday Speech, on camera
Timing: Some
Playing Off The Partner: Built On It. The pleasure of the scene is in how she listens to him, and he never takes the same tangent twice.
Freedom To Improvise: Built On It. The brief gives the beats and leaves the talk to the take, so you find each argument and tangent live with the reader.
Tone: comic to lyric, 3 changes of tone to play. An argument about clouds, a tangent worth following, a quiet admission, and a streak that keeps him up there.
Techniques It Asks: Improvisation. The talk is improvised, unhurried and found in the moment, built on whatever the reader offers.
It Only Takes: Open. With the talk left to the take, each night on the roof goes differently, so it can only be played open, never set and repeated.

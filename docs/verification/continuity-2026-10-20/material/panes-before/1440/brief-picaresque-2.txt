The Guesser Of Weights
Notes From The Overnight Bus

On an overnight bus with a dying phone, a charming drifter of fifty-one records his older sister a string of voice notes about the year’s escapades, each less likely than the last, working his way around to her spare room.

THE SCENE
Where And When
The back seat of an overnight bus between two cities, past one in the morning, his phone at nine percent. Since spring he has talked his way into and out of four jobs, and the last one ended this afternoon. His old phone cuts each voice note at two minutes, so he tells the year in installments, keeping his voice low for the sleeping passengers. He records alone at a microphone.
The Two Of You
Brother and older sister: she half raised him, still lives in the house they grew up in, and has bailed him out twice already.
You Play
Clem Harrow, fifty-one, lately by his own account a cruise-ship magician’s assistant, a mattress salesman and a ghost-tour guide.
You Speak To
His sister, asleep three hundred miles away, who’ll hear the notes over breakfast; he speaks to the phone alone.
The Genre Asks
Command Of The Frame: Status and threat carried in the body and the look more than the words: the person everyone in the room watches. A self-tape in it is judged more on connection and execution, and calls on your presence and physical expression. Rehearsing on your feet: status is found standing, in how little you need to move.
Comic Rhythm: Setup and payoff: cues picked up, pauses placed, the laugh landing where the timing puts it. A self-tape in it is judged more on responsiveness and execution, and calls on your adaptability and focus. Rehearsing on your feet: timing lives in the exchange and is found by playing it through.
The model screen picaresque was carried by its lead’s charm across an episodic, roistering story. This comes from the genre’s research, mostly held with low confidence.
What You Want
Get her to offer him the spare room for a week, without his ever having to ask.
What’s In The Way
She has heard versions of these stories all her life, the battery is dying, and asking outright would mean admitting the year has beaten him.
WHERE IT TURNS
He says a breezy hello from the back of the bus and tells how he left the cruise ship at its first port, in a hurry. (Comic)
Next comes the mattress store, where he outsold everyone for a week by napping in the window display, until the owner found him. (Comic)
Then he tells of a summer as a ghost-tour guide in a town he’d arrived in that afternoon, inventing every ghost on the walk. (Comic)
More quietly, in a fourth note, he tells of the town where he had a room of his own for a month, and why he left it. (Lyric and Grave, held at once)
He catches himself making the fourth sound better than it was, laughs, and tells it again plainly. (Comic and Grave, held at once)
With the battery nearly gone, he mentions the spare room as a joke, and the phone dies before he can take it back. (Grave and Lyric, held at once)

3 changes of tone between beats and 3 beats held two ways. Rehearsal is complete at 50% in a beat, up to 15% more in a beat a change leads into, and 8% more in one held two ways.

WHAT NOTES FROM THE OVERNIGHT BUS ASKS
Everyday Speech
Timing: Much. Each note is told with a storyteller’s timing, a setup, a pause and a twist, inside a two-minute limit he can feel running out.
Vocal Demand: Much. Six notes in one sitting, kept low for a sleeping bus, ask for range and stamina: the brag, the hush of the fourth note, and a voice that stays light to the end.
Freedom To Improvise: Some
Tone: comic to lyric to grave, 3 changes of tone to play. A breezy hello, three escapades told for laughs, one that turns out to matter, a correction told plainly, then the ask disguised as a joke.

How This Works
The Raconteur. He’s performing for his sister as he always has, and every escapade is polished to make her laugh over breakfast. It rests on: She has heard versions of these stories all her life, and the first three notes are told for laughs. Fully prepared, it would come to 55.0 for you now.
Tired Of The Road. His jokes are how he gets near the one true note, and the scene is a drifter admitting, sideways, that he wants to stop. It rests on: He tells the fourth note again plainly and ends on the spare room. Fully prepared, it would come to 55.0 for you now.
One Long Breath. Told almost without stopping, the six notes run together into one year, and the voice holds steady from the first to the last. It rests on: He records six notes in one sitting while the battery runs down. Fully prepared, it would come to 54.8 for you now.

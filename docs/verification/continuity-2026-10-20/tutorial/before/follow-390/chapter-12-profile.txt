The Player Profile shows you as an actor. Open it with Player Profile in the menu, or press your name at the bottom of the menu. Attributes And Craft lists your twelve abilities: the acting abilities Emotional Depth, Presence, Listening And Responsiveness, Imagination, Focus, Memory, Adaptability, Composure, Vocal Expression and Physical Expression, and beside them Discipline and Stamina. Each runs from 0 to 100. Press one to see what it is, how it grows and where it counts. Techniques, below it, lists your 17 techniques, the skills the school trains, and each is Mastered at 90. The traits you chose at the start are under Identity And Background, at the top: press one to see what it changes. Four different things make you better: your abilities, your techniques, your genre knowledge and practice. Your abilities are part of you: how you listen, how you hold your nerve. Your techniques are skills a class trains. Your genre knowledge is how well you know a kind of play or film. Practice is work you set yourself, outside class. Classes, coursework, rehearsals and nights on stage build what they train, up to the level classes reach. Practice takes you further, all the way to the top. To practice, press the exercise in the Practice column beside an ability or a technique, such as Comic Timing, choose a Tier and a Focus, and press Practice An Hour. Before you press it, the line above the button says about how much the hour will add, and why. The hour moves the clock on, and what it found shows under the button. Doing the same work again on the same day builds only half as much, whether it’s a class, a night or an exercise, and practice past 4 hours a day stops counting. A technique past 90 improves no result, because no piece asks more of it. A finished piece, whether a showing, a night on stage or a self-tape, is judged on the qualities your teacher looks for: believability, interpretation, connection, execution, consistency and responsiveness, as each piece calls for them. Each piece says what held it back and by how much. Your Work So Far looks across all your pieces: what they show most, and what keeps holding them back. Under Genres, Study It reads a play or watches a film of that genre, which builds your Familiarity with it. Every genre also has a practice brief, an original scene written for SLATE. Press Open Its Brief to read where and when it’s set, who the two of you are, what you want, what’s in the way and where it turns. On the brief, prepare it with Break It Down, Learn The Lines and Rehearse It On Your Feet, each with its minutes on the button. Under Your Reading, choose what you take the scene to be about and press Read It This Way. Then choose how to play it under How You’ll Play It and press Play It This Way. Once you have, Work It Through works on it as it does on a class scene. Under Read With, choose who reads the other part, a recording or a classmate who learns it as you rehearse together, and press Read With Them. When you’re ready, press Tape It: it takes an hour, and a message gives the tape’s result. A self-tape also asks you to know its genre. Your genre knowledge is the better of your Familiarity and your Work In It (your nights on stage and self-tapes in that genre), and it counts as complete at 90. A genre you’ve never met costs you at most 25% of a tape’s interpretation. What To Work On Next, on the Player Profile, names what’s most worth training now, and a brief has its own What To Work On. Each estimates what 20 hours of practice, an hour a day, would add to your recent work. It’s an estimate, not a result. Press Why This Recommendation, under it, to see what the estimate assumes and what else is worth your time. What finishing a piece’s own preparation would bring is said there apart from training, so you can tell how ready a piece is now from how ready it could be. A way of playing a scene can suit it or work against it, depending on the scene: its genre, its reader, its tones, its turns and the reading you’ve chosen. One that suits the scene is easier to make work and quicker to rehearse, and one that works against it is harder. Either can make the better tape if it plays to your strengths, and the tape tells you which did. Your tape with your reader is what a casting office would see. A Strong tape earns a callback: a harder version of the scene with a note on how to play it, which only a tape played that way answers. Loving a genre makes a rehearsal or a night in a play of that kind a better day for you, but it never makes the performance better. Read “Cover, Benefits And Returns” next. It opens Papers: your health cover, benefits and tax returns. The Details Discipline grows with each day you practice and each call you keep, and it makes everything classes, nights, preparation steps and practice build count a little more, or a little less. Practice doesn’t train Stamina: Stamina is your fitness, built by Work Out, under Look After Yourself, and by a class that works the body as hard, Movement Invention or stage combat. Some abilities make a session get more done: memory when you learn lines, imagination at the table and in a brief’s breakdown, focus when you get to know a scene, listening when you rehearse with a partner or a reader or rehearse a brief with its reader, and adaptability when you revise after a note or work on a director’s note about the blocking. Which abilities count most depends on the piece. A long text calls on memory, high stakes on composure, little of a partner to play off on presence, many beats on discipline, a grave piece on emotional depth, a comic one on focus, and an invented world on imagination. A night in front of an audience calls on presence, memory, composure and discipline as well, and on stamina according to how long and hard it is. With every ability at full, every technique mastered, the preparation complete and you rested and well, a piece comes to 100. A self-tape counts toward your Work In It for its genre according to how prepared it was: one taped cold counts 15% as much as a prepared one. Where research has established what a genre asks of an actor, its brief stages that technique, and the brief shows it before you begin. Each genre is played in its own register, one or two concerns such as feeling held in, comic rhythm or belief in what isn’t there. The Genre Asks names them on its brief; each pulls the tape toward what it asks for, and a tape held back by an ability one of them calls on says so. What To Work On can recommend something other than what held your last tape back most, and it says when two things are about as useful. Under Why This Recommendation, it says what its estimate assumes about your tapes and their genres, names any it counts as fully prepared, and says whether preparation, training or both are worth your time now. When you come back to it, it says how long its recommendation has stood, what has changed since and what changed it, and what to do next. A tape is judged beat by beat: how well each beat is rehearsed, how far into the tape it comes, where your stamina starts to show, and any slip in the beat before, which composure and adaptability help you recover from. Your own result is yours alone, whoever reads with you.

  What it asks of an actor.   Why it’s applied, in the research’s words: genre-wide: Tasker’s encyclopedia entry states at the level of the whole form that action and adventure cinema foregrounds the body in motion and in combat, which is a genre-level convention of on-screen combat. One credible source, so medium, not high. Doing one’s own stunts (Fairbanks, Flynn) remains a swashbuckler-star matter and isn’t what the tag is applied for. What the research says: Action and Adventure Films: "as a form that foregrounds the body in motion and in combat, action and adventure cinema advances a physical (frequently sexualized), imagery of heroism"; "the action or adventure hero is called upon to demonstrate courage, initiative and physical endurance"; the swashbuckler hero is shown "displaying martial skills in extravagant scenes of swordplay"; Flynn’s "athletic performance came to define the romantic male exuberance of the swashbuckler." Douglas Fairbanks: No Stuntman Required: Fairbanks "completed most stunts himself" and secretly did the Robin Hood drawbridge stunt after agreeing to a double. Makes no claim about swashbuckler stars in general. Swordsmen of the Screen: From Douglas Fairbanks to Michael York: Quoted (via Wikipedia, not read directly) as saying that to understand a swashbuckler 'we must look at the art director, costume designer, fencing master, stunt arranger, cinematographer and actor'. The book’s existence and authorship are confirmed by the Wikipedia citation; the quote itself wasn’t seen on a fetched page. (two of its three sources read directly and the remaining one from a search summary, reviewed 2026-10-10; medium confidence). Still uncertain: Tasker treats action and adventure as one form; scholars such as Brian Taves separate historical adventure from action, and some adventure films (travel or exploration stories) have little combat. Only one source states the genre-level point directly. The Richards quote wasn’t read at source.

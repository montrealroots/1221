## Yakuza (yakuza; low confidence; not applied; belongs elsewhere)
  What it asks of an actor. The 1960s ninkyo (chivalry) yakuza film formulaically climaxed in the hero’s katana showdown against a rival gang. The 1970s jitsuroku cycle replaced it with chaotic, unheroic brawls and gunplay. Sources describe the fights, not actors' training.
  Why it adds no requirement. What the research found (Fights And Stunts) belongs to the lead’s climactic sword fight in ninkyo eiga, the chivalry yakuza films of the 1960s, not to the genre as a whole, so the genre adds no requirement of its own.
  What the research says: Tradition and Modernity in Japanese Yakuza Films of the 1960s and 70s: Ninkyo climaxes feature 'the hero armed only with a traditional katana sword slicing his way through a gang of bad yakuza with guns'; in Battles Without Honor and Humanity yakuza’s 'hands shake when they fire a gun'; 'a free-for-all brawl, with Tetsuya Watari fighting in a downpour'. Yakuza Films at the Asia Society: 1960s yakuza films were 'always climaxing in a showdown where the anti-hero takes down all nemeses with a katana', 'a bloody ballet of sword swinging'. 10 great Japanese gangster films: Ken Takakura as 'the stoic, tattooed, katana-wielding tough guy'; Fukasaku’s jitsuroku style 'stripped away the mythos and surface sheen of the old-school ninkyo eiga of the 1960s'. (all three of its sources read directly, reviewed 2026-10-10; low confidence). Still uncertain: The sources are film criticism about screen content, not about what actors trained for. A whole-genre combat demand might be argued from violence across cycles, but no source says it, and much later yakuza cinema (Kitano’s, for one) isn’t led by fight choreography.

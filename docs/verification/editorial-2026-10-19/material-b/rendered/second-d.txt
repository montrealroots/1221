 Postmarked Thursday
A practice brief for Magical Realism: an original scene written for SLATE, taped to a reader off camera. The Back Bedroom Postmarked Thursday 
A mail carrier in a hill town brings a man a letter from his estranged sister, postmarked two days from now, and tries to get him to open it before she’s written it.
The Scene
Where And When
A doorstep on the steepest street of a hill town, a Tuesday morning in late spring. Here a letter now and then arrives before it’s sent, and everyone knows the rule: one left sealed past the date on its postmark goes back, and never gets written. This one is postmarked Thursday, in his sister’s hand.
The Two Of You
She has carried his mail for nine years and they’ve never talked past the weather; she knows his sister’s handwriting from the birthday cards that stopped coming.
You Play
Ines Carvalho, fifty, the upper-route carrier, who has delivered early letters before and treats them like any parcel that needs a signature.
The Reader Plays
Teodor Lusk, sixties, in his bathrobe behind a half-open door, who hasn’t spoken to his sister in eleven years.
How They Play It
Withholding: playing opposite it, the tape counts connection for more and leans on your listening and responsiveness.
The Genre Asks
Played Straight: Meant completely: the feeling played with full conviction, never winked at, however large or strange the material. A self-tape in it is judged more on believability and connection, and calls on your emotional depth and presence. The breakdown and the lines: believing it starts with knowing exactly what your character wants and why it matters to them.
Belief In What Isn’t There: An invented world made concrete: the creature, the machine or the magic played as specifically as a cup of tea. A self-tape in it is judged more on believability and interpretation, and calls on your imagination. The breakdown: the world’s rules have to be worked out before they can be believed.
Fantastic elements presented in a matter-of-fact way, the marvel never remarked on. This comes from the genre’s research, mostly held with low confidence.
What You Want
Get him to open the envelope on the doorstep today, so his sister goes on to write it.
What’s In The Way
He refuses to read words his sister hasn’t chosen yet, and he gives the carrier almost nothing but a door held half shut.
Where It Turns
She explains the postmark the way she’d explain postage due, briskly, holding the envelope on her side of the frame. (Comic)
He says he’ll send it back; she tells him what happens to a letter that goes back early. (Grave)
She tells him the handwriting on the envelope is steadier than on the last card, years ago, and that she’d know. (Lyric and Grave, held at once)
He opens the door a little wider; she holds the letter out toward his eyeline and waits. (Lyric)
3 changes of tone between beats and 1 beat held two ways. Rehearsal is complete at 50% in a beat, up to 15% more in a beat a change leads into, and 8% more in one held two ways. Postal business told as routine, the rule that makes it urgent, the handwriting that makes it personal, then a wait that leaves the choice with him.
What Postmarked Thursday Asks
Everyday Speech, on camera
Timing: Some
Playing Off The Partner: Built On It. He gives her a half-open door and a few words, and every new argument she tries is built from the last thing he let slip.
Vocal Demand: Some
Tone: comic to grave to lyric, 3 transitions to play. Postal business told as routine, the rule that makes it urgent, the handwriting that makes it personal, then a wait that leaves the choice with him.

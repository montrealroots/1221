 The Third Telling
A practice brief for Psychological Thriller: an original scene written for SLATE, taped to a reader off camera. The Rehung Frames The Third Telling 
Asked by a detective to tell the night her husband disappeared for the third time, a woman tells it in exactly the same words, and the detective wants to know why.
The Scene
Where And When
An interview room at a county sheriff’s office, a Tuesday afternoon, three days after her husband walked out of their lake house and didn’t come back. She has told the story twice already, and both times it was word for word the same, down to the time on the stove clock. The detective has noticed. She isn’t under arrest and can leave whenever she likes. For the tape, sit at the table, with the reader off camera across it as the detective.
The Two Of You
Strangers until Saturday: he’s polite and patient and doesn’t believe a word.
You Play
Dana Whitlock, forty-three, a court stenographer, who remembers everything she hears exactly and always has.
The Reader Plays
Detective Ray Corso, who asks the same questions in a different order every time.
How They Play It
Pressing: playing opposite it, the tape counts responsiveness for more and leans on your adaptability.
The Genre Asks
Feeling Held In: The feeling is there and kept under: stillness, economy, what isn’t said carrying what is. A self-tape in it is judged more on consistency and believability, and calls on your composure and focus. Learning the lines and rehearsing on your feet: control comes from knowing it well enough to hold it still.
Dense Text, Exact: A lot of words, or difficult ones, delivered fluently and at speed: jargon, argument or deduction as second nature. A self-tape in it is judged more on execution and interpretation, and calls on your memory and focus. Learning the lines: fluency at speed is the lines learned past knowing them.
A self-concealing lead played as clinical and fully worked out in advance. This comes from the genre’s research, mostly held with low confidence.
What You Want
Get through the third telling without giving him anything new, and leave.
What’s In The Way
He changes the order of everything, her exactness sounds rehearsed when it’s only how her memory has always worked, and there’s one detail she has left out all three times.
Where It Turns
She begins the story again, with the same first sentence and the stove clock at ten past nine. (Grave)
He starts her in the middle; she goes back to the beginning, politely, and goes on. (Grave and Comic, held at once)
He reads her own words back from the first interview, and she corrects him on one word. (Grave)
He asks why her story never changes; she explains her work, exactly and at length. (Grave)
He asks about the boat, and for the first time she uses a word she hasn’t used before. (Grotesque and Grave, held at once)
She stands, says she’d like to leave now, and does, without hurrying. (Grave)
4 changes of tone between beats and 2 beats held two ways. Rehearsal is complete at 50% in a beat, up to 15% more in a beat a change leads into, and 8% more in one held two ways. The same beginning, an interruption refused, a correction, an explanation, the one new word, then leaving.
What The Third Telling Asks
Everyday Speech, on camera
Timing: Built On It. The telling is word for word each time: she has to meet every interruption and take the story up again exactly where she left it.
Playing Off The Partner: Much. He asks the same questions in a different order every time, and each answer is shaped to where he has jumped.
Vocal Demand: Some
Tone: grave to comic to grotesque, 4 transitions to play. The same beginning, an interruption refused, a correction, an explanation, the one new word, then leaving.

A practice brief for Queer Cinema: an original scene written for SLATE, taped to a reader off camera. The Toast The Plaque In The Trophy Case 
A retired mail carrier who came out as a trans woman last spring asks her bowling league’s secretary to put her name on the championship plaque she won in 1998.
The Scene
Where And When
The back office of a bowling alley on league night, a Tuesday in November, the pins clattering through the wall. Above the desk is the trophy case, and in it the plaque from the 1998 city championship, engraved with the name she used then. Rosalind rejoined the league in September and has walked past it every week since. The engraver’s order form is in her purse, already filled in.
The Two Of You
Teammates for eleven seasons in the nineties, the kind who drove each other to the hospital and never once talked about feelings. Since she came back, Hank has been kind and careful, and he still sometimes gets her name wrong and apologizes.
You Play
Rosalind Petrakis, sixty-three, a retired mail carrier with a 190 average and a hook the younger bowlers still ask her about.
The Reader Plays
Hank Duvall, seventy, league secretary for thirty years, at his desk with the week’s score sheets.
How They Play It
Steady: playing opposite it asks nothing more than the scene does.
The Genre Asks
Played Straight: Meant completely: the feeling played with full conviction, never winked at, however large or strange the material. A self-tape in it is judged more on believability and connection, and calls on your emotional depth and presence. The breakdown and the lines: believing it starts with knowing exactly what your character wants and why it matters to them.
Played Between Two: The scene lives between the two of you: chemistry, contrast, a part each that only works against the other. A self-tape in it is judged more on connection and responsiveness, and calls on your presence, listening and responsiveness and emotional depth. Rehearsing on your feet: a partnership is built in the exchange, played through until each part answers the other.
Defined by its subjects: lives and relationships played truthfully. The research found nothing on how it’s played, so this is estimated from its conventions.
What You Want

What’s In The Way

He takes out his pen and asks how she spells Rosalind, and she spells it for him slowly, and they both laugh. (Lyric and Comic, held at once)
3 changes of tone between beats and 2 beats held two ways. Rehearsal is complete at 50% in a beat, up to 15% more in a beat a change leads into, and 8% more in one held two ways. Small talk with the ask under it, a stumble she waits out, a plain account of what the plaque costs her, then a signature that becomes a joke between old teammates.
What The Plaque In The Trophy Case Asks
Everyday Speech, on camera
Timing: Some
Playing Off The Partner: Built On It. She can move only as far as Hank can come with her, and every hesitation of his tells her how far he’ll go.
Tone: comic to grave to lyric, 3 transitions to play. Small talk with the ask under it, a stumble she waits out, a plain account of what the plaque costs her, then a signature that becomes a joke between old teammates.

A practice brief for Revenge: an original scene written for SLATE, taped to a reader off camera. The Retirement Folder The Dealer’s Last Hand 
Eight years after she lost her diner to a stacked deck, a card dealer plays the man who stacked it heads-up for the deed, with a deck he insists be fresh.
The Scene
Where And When
The back room of a lumberyard office, past two in the morning, the private Thursday game over and the other players gone home. Eight years ago, the week her divorce came through, June lost her diner to Royce Garland at this table on a hand he’d stacked. She got sober, learned to deal at a casino two hundred miles away, and was hired for his game in March. Tonight, bored and up big, he’s offered to play the dealer heads-up: her savings against the deed to the diner. He still owns it and has let it fall apart. For the tape, sit at the table’s edge with the deck and the chips on your side of the frame, and deal his cards to an empty place at the frame’s edge; he never touches what she holds.
The Two Of You
Dealer and player, as far as he knows. He took her diner in a single night and has never once looked closely at the woman who shuffles his cards.
You Play
June Halloran, forty-six, eight years sober, with the steadiest hands in the county and her savings on the table.
The Reader Plays
Royce Garland, sixty, who owns the lumberyard, the game and half of Main Street, and who watches every dealer’s hands.
How They Play It
Pressing: playing opposite it, the tape counts responsiveness for more and leans on your adaptability.
The Genre Asks
States At The Limit: Fear, grief or exhaustion at their extreme, sustained, and believed though the threat is often not in the room. A self-tape in it is judged more on believability and execution, and calls on your emotional depth and physical expression. The lines, then rehearsal on your feet: an extreme state has to be found in the body, on lines you no longer think about.
Command Of The Frame: Status and threat carried in the body and the look more than the words: the person everyone in the room watches. A self-tape in it is judged more on connection and execution, and calls on your presence and physical expression. Rehearsing on your feet: status is found standing, in how little you need to move.
Months of fight training to carry a protagonist driven by a grievous wrong. This comes from the genre’s research, mostly held with low confidence.
What You Want

What’s In The Way

Where It Turns

He tells her, laughing, about the night he won a diner on a pair of nines from a woman too drunk to see the deal. (Grotesque)
He calls for a fresh deck; she breaks the seal in front of him and shuffles it slowly, so he can watch every card. (Grave)
He studies her hands and asks where she learned to deal like that; she tells him the casino and the town, and leaves out the rest. (Grave)
She turns his cards up at his word and he calls the hand won; she turns hers over card by card, and asks him if he remembers what the diner was called. (Grave and Lyric, held at once)
3 changes of tone between beats and 1 beat held two ways. Rehearsal is complete at 50% in a beat, up to 15% more in a beat a change leads into, and 8% more in one held two ways. An invisible dealer, his boast heard out, a fresh deck handled in plain sight, a truth told by halves, then the cards turned.
What The Dealer’s Last Hand Asks
Everyday Speech, on camera
Timing: Much. The shuffle, the deal and her answers keep a dealer’s steady rhythm under his pushing, and she turns the last cards one at a time, placing each one.
Physical Work: Much. She handles the cards all the way through: the riffle, the cut, the deal and the slow turn, with a real deck on her side of the frame and his cards dealt to an empty place at its edge.
Playing Off The Partner: Much. He presses her with a story, a demand and a question, and she has to take each one without the shuffle missing a beat.
Tone: comic to grotesque to grave to lyric, 3 transitions to play. An invisible dealer, his boast heard out, a fresh deck handled in plain sight, a truth told by halves, then the cards turned.

 The Lantern On The Ferry
A practice brief for Fairy Tale: an original scene written for SLATE, recorded alone at a microphone. One Thing She Doesn’t Know The Lantern On The Ferry 
On the one night a year it can speak, an old ferry lantern has until the midnight bell to talk the ferrywoman’s daughter out of selling the boat for a purse of fairy gold.
The Scene
Where And When
A river crossing on Midwinter Night, between the first star and the midnight bell: the only hours in the year the lantern can speak, and then only in rhyme. Linnet’s mother, the ferrywoman, died in the autumn, the crossing is behind on its tolls, and this evening a smiling stranger in a gray coat offered Linnet a heavy purse for the boat. The lantern has seen that purse before, a hundred winters ago, and by dawn it held only leaves. You record it alone at a microphone for an animated tale, before any of it is drawn.
The Two Of You
The lantern has hung over the bow since before Linnet was born and watched her learn to pole the boat; she has never once heard it speak.
You Play
Old Wick, the ferry lantern, tin and glass and very old, fond of the girl and fonder of its own rhymes.
You Speak To
Linnet, sixteen, the ferrywoman’s daughter. Her lines aren’t in the booth, so play to her as if she stood on the landing.
The Genre Asks
Belief In What Isn’t There: An invented world made concrete: the creature, the machine or the magic played as specifically as a cup of tea. A self-tape in it is judged more on believability and interpretation, and calls on your imagination. The breakdown: the world’s rules have to be worked out before they can be believed.
Played Large: Feeling worn on the outside and sized to fill the frame: the voice and the body carry it as much as the face. A self-tape in it is judged more on execution and connection, and calls on your vocal expression, physical expression and presence. Rehearsing on your feet: a size the body and the voice can carry has to be found aloud and standing.
A wonder tale: marvels made real, told with the clear shapes of the folktale. The research found nothing on how it’s played, so this is estimated from its conventions.
What You Want
Stop the sale before the stranger comes back for his answer at midnight.
What’s In The Way
Linnet thinks she’s dreaming, the lantern can speak only in rhyme, and the gold looks like the only way to keep the crossing.
Where It Turns
The lantern clears its throat and introduces itself in a grand couplet, and Linnet nearly drops it in the river. (Comic)
She tests it with questions only the lantern could answer, and it answers each one in rhyme, showing off. (Comic and Lyric, held at once)
It tells her where the gray coat’s gold has gone before, and what the last ferryman found in his purse at dawn. (Grave)
The bell begins, and with the rhymes it has left it tells her the boat was always her mother’s, and now it’s hers. (Lyric)
3 changes of tone between beats and 1 beat held two ways. Rehearsal is complete at 50% in a beat, up to 15% more in a beat a change leads into, and 8% more in one held two ways. A grand introduction played for laughs, a test it shows off in, a warning, and a goodbye against the bell.
What The Lantern On The Ferry Asks
Verse: The lantern can speak only in rhyme, short couplets with a strong beat, so every line has a shape to keep and the sense has to come through it for a girl who thinks she’s dreaming.
Timing: Much. A rhyme lands on its last word, and the bell in the last beat counts down the lines the lantern has left, so the pace tightens as its hours run out.
Playing Off The Partner: Some
Vocal Demand: Built On It. One small, old, vain voice carries a joke, a warning and a goodbye, with no face to help it.
Tone: comic to lyric to grave, 3 transitions to play. A grand introduction played for laughs, a test it shows off in, a warning, and a goodbye against the bell.
Techniques It Asks: Voice-Over. You voice it alone at a microphone for an animated tale, so it’s all voice-over: the lantern’s age, its vanity and its love for the girl, played to a partner who isn’t in the booth.

On career day in her son’s third-grade class, a sanitation truck driver who swapped two shifts to be there has twenty minutes to win over a rug full of eight-year-olds, and a son who asked her not to mention the smell.
The Scene
Where And When

Last night her son asked her to say she works “for the city” and leave it there. She has brought her reflective vest, her work gloves and a laminated card of the hand signals she uses to back the truck down a narrow street. For the tape, set the vest, the gloves and the card on a chair at your side, in frame.

You Play

The Reader Plays

A village healer has to win passage through a mountain pass for a feverish child from its warden, a spirit who forgets every word said to it a minute after it’s said.
The Scene
Where And When
The gate of a high mountain pass at nightfall in deep winter. The warden holds nothing longer than a minute: every agreement, every name and every price slips away, and it greets whoever stands before it as a stranger. The child lies on a sled at her back, off frame, wrapped in every blanket the village had.
The Two Of You
Strangers every minute: she has stood before the warden twice before, as a girl and as a bride, and it has never once known her.
You Play

The Reader Plays

A practice brief for Farce: an original scene written for SLATE, taped to a reader off camera. The Second Cake Rooms 412, 414 And 416 
A hotel night manager who promised the same suite to three guests keeps all three content for one night by running between their doors, until all three doors open at once.
The Scene
Where And When
The fourth-floor corridor of a grand old hotel during a convention, a little before midnight, three doors in a row. Strangers who each expect the best the hotel has, and the hotel’s last chance of a good review.
You Play
Felix Brand, forty, the night manager, who has never once been caught and believes he never will be.

What’s In The Way

At closing time in 1947, a pawnbroker who knows what the revolver in his safe was used for is pressed by the man who pawned it to hand it back without the ticket, and tells us the night afterward in his own words.
The Scene
Where And When
A pawnshop on a wet side street in 1947, ten minutes to closing, the blinds half down. Three days ago a man pawned a revolver; yesterday the papers said a bookmaker was shot with one like it. The tape opens and closes with his narration, the account he gives afterward, which you record off camera in the same session to lay over the scene. For the tape, keep the counter, the ledger and the safe’s dial on your side of the frame.
The Two Of You

You Play

How They Play It
Pressing: playing opposite it, the tape counts responsiveness for more and leans on your adaptability.
The Genre Asks

A self-tape in it is judged more on connection and execution, and calls on your presence and physical expression. Rehearsing on your feet: status is found standing, in how little you need to move.
Feeling Held In: The feeling is there and kept under: stillness, economy, what isn’t said carrying what is. A self-tape in it is judged more on consistency and believability, and calls on your composure and focus. Learning the lines and rehearsing on your feet: control comes from knowing it well enough to hold it still.

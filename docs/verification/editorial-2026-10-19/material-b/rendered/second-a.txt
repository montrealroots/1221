=== brief-action-2
 The Safe Behind The Ring
A practice brief for Action: an original scene written for SLATE, taped to a reader off camera. The Loading Bay The Safe Behind The Ring 
A boxing coach locking up late finds the best student she ever trained prying open the office safe, and has to get him out of her gym empty-handed without either of them getting hurt.
The Scene
Where And When
A small boxing gym above a hardware store, at eleven at night, the ring lights off and only the office lamp on. The safe holds a month of members’ dues, this month’s rent among them. He owes money to men who don’t wait, and he came on the one night she was meant to be at her sister’s.
The Two Of You
Coach and pupil for eight years: she found him at fourteen picking fights outside the station. He walked out after a loss last spring and hasn’t answered her calls since.
You Play
Coach Imelda Ruiz, fifty-four, a former featherweight champion with a shoulder that never healed right.
The Reader Plays
Teo, twenty-three, her best student once, hood up, crouched at the safe just off frame.
How They Play It
Pressing: playing opposite it, the tape counts responsiveness for more and leans on your adaptability.
The Genre Asks
Command Of The Frame: Status and threat carried in the body and the look more than the words: the person everyone in the room watches. A self-tape in it is judged more on connection and execution, and calls on your presence and physical expression. Rehearsing on your feet: status is found standing, in how little you need to move.
Suspense Held: A threat kept alive under every line: what’s at stake never dropped, even in the quiet. A self-tape in it is judged more on consistency and interpretation, and calls on your focus and listening and responsiveness. The breakdown, then rehearsal: suspense is held by knowing at every moment what your character fears will happen, and keeping it alive through the scene.
Its chases and fights are staged techniques; between them the scenes turn on who holds the frame and what the threat is. The research found nothing on how it’s played, so this is estimated from its conventions.
What You Want
Make him drop the pry bar and go down the front stairs with his pockets empty.
What’s In The Way
He’s younger and faster, he knows which shoulder is the bad one, and he fears the men he owes more than he fears her.
Where It Turns
She switches on the ring lights and says his name, and he doesn’t stop working at the lock. (Grave)
She talks about his first amateur bout, the one he won on a split decision, to keep him listening. (Grave and Lyric, held at once)
He rushes her to reach the stairs; the fight is four marked moves, and the first one catches her bad shoulder. (Grave)
She slips his last swing and has him beaten, her right hand cocked, and she doesn’t throw it. (Grave)
He tells her who he owes and how much, and she laughs, because it’s less than the new roof cost. (Comic and Grave, held at once)
She opens the safe herself, counts the debt out onto the desk, and tells him he starts sweeping the gym at six tomorrow. (Lyric)
4 changes of tone between beats and 2 beats held two ways. Rehearsal is complete at 50% in a beat, up to 15% more in a beat a change leads into, and 8% more in one held two ways. A slow start in the half dark, a memory used to hold him, a fight she wins by not finishing it, then a laugh and a debt paid in work.
What The Safe Behind The Ring Asks
Everyday Speech, on camera
Timing: Much. The fight’s four moves sit inside the talk, and each move cuts off the line before it, so you have to place exactly where the words stop.
Physical Work: Built On It. She plays the scene in a body that still knows how to fight and a shoulder that can’t: the stance, the four moves, the punch held back, all on her side of the frame.
Playing Off The Partner: Built On It. She reads him the way she read him from the corner, and every turn of the scene answers something he does or says.
Vocal Demand: Some
Tone: grave to lyric to comic, 4 transitions to play. A slow start in the half dark, a memory used to hold him, a fight she wins by not finishing it, then a laugh and a debt paid in work.
Techniques It Asks: Stage Combat. The fight is staged combat. Mark its four moves solo, at reduced speed, against the reader’s called cues from off camera, with no contact. React to the hit on her shoulder; it never lands. The pry bar stays off frame with him and is only talked about.

A practice brief for Absurdist: an original scene written for SLATE, taped to a reader off camera. Proof Of Arrival The Answer On Page Four 
At a job interview, a man finds the interviewer has every one of his answers already typed in a folder, a page ahead of him, and tries to say one thing the folder doesn’t have.
The Scene
Where And When
A windowless interview room on the ninth floor of an insurance company, at ten past nine on a Monday. The post is junior filing clerk. The interviewer opened a thick folder with the man’s name on the cover before he sat down, and has been reading from it ever since.
The Two Of You
Interviewer and candidate, who shared an elevator a minute ago. The interviewer has never known the folder to be wrong and finds that comforting.
You Play
Arno Vasko, forty, who has applied for this post and nineteen others this year, in his only suit.
The Reader Plays
The interviewer, mild and courteous, turning the folder’s pages and reading each answer aloud a moment before he gives it.
How They Play It
Unpredictable: playing opposite it, the tape counts responsiveness and consistency for more and leans on your adaptability and composure.
The Genre Asks
The World’s Own Logic: A world with rules of its own, absurd, dreamlike or satirical, played wholeheartedly by them and never commented on. A self-tape in it is judged more on interpretation and execution, and calls on your imagination and physical expression. The breakdown: the world’s logic has to be found before it can be played as if it were sense.
Comic Rhythm: Setup and payoff: cues picked up, pauses placed, the laugh landing where the timing puts it. A self-tape in it is judged more on responsiveness and execution, and calls on your adaptability and focus. Rehearsing on your feet: timing lives in the exchange and is found by playing it through.
Beckett’s drama asks for exact control of timing, rhythm and timed silence inside a world whose logic is distorted. This comes from the genre’s research, mostly held with low confidence.
What You Want
Say one thing, any one thing, that isn’t typed in the folder.
What’s In The Way
Whatever he tries turns up on the next page, and the interviewer is only ever pleased to find it there.
Where It Turns
He answers the first question, and the interviewer reads his answer aloud half a second before he reaches the end of it. (Comic)
He tries a lie, then nonsense, then saying nothing at all; each is on the next page, and the interviewer reads out the silence too. (Comic and Grotesque, held at once)
He asks what it says at the end. The interviewer reads: “He thanks us and leaves.” He sits a long while, and then he thanks them. (Grave)

What The Answer On Page Four Asks
Everyday Speech, on camera
Timing: Built On It. The interviewer’s reading lands just ahead of each answer, so every line is a race the man loses by a hair, and the comedy depends on how small the hair is.
Physical Work: Some
Playing Off The Partner: Built On It. Everything he tries is a reply to what the interviewer has just read, and the order of the pages decides what he has to answer next.
Freedom To Improvise: Much. The reader picks which typed answer to read next, in any order and never the same twice, so you find his attempts to get ahead fresh on each take.
Tone: comic to grotesque to grave, 2 transitions to play. An ordinary interview going strange, a frantic effort that turns grotesque, then a quiet surrender to the page.

On her first day as a bodyguard, a former stunt performer has to walk a nineteen-year-old video-game streamer down a hotel’s service corridor to the loading dock while he livestreams the trip to his followers.

The service corridor of a convention hotel at ten at night, between the laundry and the loading dock. Three hundred fans are packed around the front entrance, so the plan is the back way. The agency put her on the detail this morning because his usual guard has the flu. A practice brief for Alternate History: an original scene written for SLATE, recorded alone at a microphone. The Receiver Column Nine 
In a world where a treaty outlawed the electronic computer before the first one was finished, the head of a forecast hall where two hundred people compute the weather by hand finds an error ninety minutes before a storm warning must go out, and talks the whole hall through the work again over the loudspeaker.
The Scene
Where And When
The forecast hall of the national weather service in 1969, in a world where a treaty signed in 1946 forbade any machine that calculates electronically, and none was ever built. Two hundred people at tiered desks work tomorrow’s weather by hand and by mechanical adding machine, one patch of map each, and the head of the hall speaks to them from a gallery microphone. A storm is coming up the coast, and the warning has to reach the radio stations by six. At half past four she finds that column nine, the southern pressure figures, carried a wrong number from the first sheet. You record alone at a microphone, and the hall is only on screen.
The Two Of You
She came up through the desks herself and knows most of the two hundred by their handwriting. They trust her voice more than any memo from upstairs.
You Play
Odile Brandt, fifty, head of the forecast hall, twenty-six years on its floor and its gallery.
You Speak To
The hall’s two hundred computers, the people at the desks below the gallery. Their figures come up to you on paper slips, and none of them is in the booth.
The Genre Asks
Another Age’s Manners: How people of another time stood, moved, spoke and kept their place, kept up without looking like effort. A self-tape in it is judged more on execution and interpretation, and calls on your physical expression, discipline and vocal expression. The breakdown and rehearsal together: the manners are researched first, then made habit on your feet.
Belief In What Isn’t There: An invented world made concrete: the creature, the machine or the magic played as specifically as a cup of tea. A self-tape in it is judged more on believability and interpretation, and calls on your imagination. The breakdown: the world’s rules have to be worked out before they can be believed.
A past that didn’t happen: another age’s manners, and a world changed by one event, both to be made concrete. The research found nothing on how it’s played, so this is estimated from its conventions.

The Scene
Where And When
An animated feature: the lamp room of a lighthouse on a rock, in a storm. The director reads Tuck’s lines off camera.
The Two Of You
Tuck has followed him for a week, and Barnaby has told the chick to go home every day of it.
You Play
Barnaby, the walrus, old, enormous, slow on land and proud of the lamp.
The Reader Plays

What You Want

What’s In The Way
His flippers won’t do it, he hates asking, and Tuck won’t stop asking questions long enough to listen.

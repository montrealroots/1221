A practice brief for Stoner Comedy: an original scene written for SLATE, taped to a reader off camera. The Wrong Couch The Call With Her Father 
A man who ate his roommate’s brownies an hour before his first video call with his girlfriend’s father has to get through twenty minutes of polite questions as they take hold.
The Scene
Where And When
A spare room in a shared rental, eight on a Sunday, a laptop propped on a stack of textbooks. His girlfriend is on a flight home and set up the call so her father could meet him before the wedding. At seven he ate two brownies from the counter for breakfast; the roommate’s warning note was stuck to the bottom of the tray. The laptop sits just below the lens, so the father’s face on its screen is the eyeline.
The Two Of You
Strangers about to be family: the father has heard about him for two years and seen one photograph.
You Play
Callum Breck, twenty-seven, a careful, well-meaning sound engineer who has never been high before and can’t tell yet whether this counts.
The Reader Plays
Dr. Henrik Salo, a retired bridge engineer, warm and thorough, whose questions jump without warning from the weather to Callum’s plans for children.
How They Play It
Unpredictable: playing opposite it, the tape counts responsiveness and consistency for more and leans on your adaptability and composure.
The Genre Asks
Comic Rhythm: Setup and payoff: cues picked up, pauses placed, the laugh landing where the timing puts it. A self-tape in it is judged more on responsiveness and execution, and calls on your adaptability and focus. Rehearsing on your feet: timing lives in the exchange and is found by playing it through.
Caught Live: Behavior that looks unrehearsed: real listening, the next line taken from what the other person just did. A self-tape in it is judged more on connection and responsiveness, and calls on your listening and responsiveness and adaptability. Rehearsing on your feet: what looks unrehearsed is found by playing it through until nothing is set but the wants.
A comedy, its timing loose and its scenes hanging out. The research found nothing on how it’s played, so this is estimated from its conventions.
What You Want
Make a good impression on the man whose blessing his girlfriend wants.
What’s In The Way
The brownies arrive in waves, time stretches between one question and the next, and the father notices everything.
Where It Turns
He opens with the answers he rehearsed, a little too fast, and compliments the bookshelf behind the father twice. (Comic)
Halfway through a question about his job the brownies take hold, and he becomes certain the father’s face is slowly coming closer to the screen. (Comic and Grotesque, held at once)
The father asks what he loves about his daughter, and the answer comes out slow, honest and much too long. (Comic and Lyric, held at once)
The father asks him plainly whether he’s feeling well, Callum tells him about the brownies, and the father laughs for a long time. (Comic)
3 changes of tone between beats and 2 beats held two ways. Rehearsal is complete at 50% in a beat, up to 15% more in a beat a change leads into, and 8% more in one held two ways. A rehearsed start, the brownies arriving in the middle of a sentence, an answer too honest to be funny and too long not to be, then the confession and the laugh that lets him off.
What The Call With Her Father Asks
Everyday Speech, on camera
Timing: Built On It. The laughs are in his delays, a pause held a beat too long or an answer that lands on the question before last, and he has to place each one on purpose against whatever the reader has just asked.
Physical Work: Much. The brownies are played in his body, small and exact: the slow blink, a hand that forgets it’s holding a mug, a lean toward a screen that seems to lean back. All of it is seated and inside the frame.
Playing Off The Partner: Much. Every laugh starts from something the father says, and Callum has to take it in a moment late and visibly.
Freedom To Improvise: Much. The reader varies the father’s small talk from take to take, inside the brief’s four turns, so Callum answers questions he hasn’t heard before and keeps the scene’s shape.
Tone: comic to grotesque to lyric, 3 transitions to play. A rehearsed start, the brownies arriving in the middle of a sentence, an answer too honest to be funny and too long not to be, then the confession and the laugh that lets him off.
Techniques It Asks: Comedy, Improvisation. It’s played for laughs, with comic timing held inside a real, rising panic. The small talk changes each take, and he has to improvise his answers in character, live.

The city’s strongest hero has to walk an assessor from the city’s repair fund through last night’s fight, move by move, to get the bakery she flattened rebuilt.
The Scene

The Two Of You

You Play

How They Play It
Steady: playing opposite it asks nothing more than the scene does.
The Genre Asks
Command Of The Frame: Status and threat carried in the body and the look more than the words: the person everyone in the room watches. What The Bakery On Calder Street Asks
Everyday Speech, on camera
Timing: Much. The jokes land between his questions and her answers, and the reenactment runs on his called cues.
Physical Work: Much. She tells the fight again with her whole body, the duck, the grab and the throw, at half speed and far too big for a meeting room.
Playing Off The Partner: Much. He’s patient and even, so every question he asks lands at full weight, and she has to take each one in before she answers it.
Tone: comic to grave to lyric, 2 transitions to play. A hero’s version, the reenactment, the moment it stops being funny, then a plain question from a woman who has stopped performing.

The reenactment is stage combat: mark its three moves solo at half speed against the reader’s called cues, with no one in front of you and nothing thrown, so it repeats safely on every take. A violinist whose audience hasn’t stopped applauding in three days tries every remedy her stage manager can think of to make them stop, and finds the one that works.

Steady: playing opposite it asks nothing more than the scene does.
The Genre Asks

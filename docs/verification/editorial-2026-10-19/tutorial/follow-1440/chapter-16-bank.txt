Your money is kept at a real bank in the country you live in, under the names that bank gives its accounts, and each account has a number written the way the country writes one. Open the bank with the button named for it on the Money line in Your Affairs on Home, or from Money in the menu. Its tabs are Accounts, Cards, Payments And Cash, Borrowing, and Statements. Move Money, on Accounts, moves money between your own accounts. Between two at the same bank it moves at once. To another bank it takes the sending bank’s time, or its country’s where the bank sets none, and it shows as Money In Transit until it lands; where the country’s banks pay instantly, that’s straight away. Money in transit is pending: it has left one account and isn’t in the other yet, so it isn’t available to spend. An account that earns interest builds it each day and is paid at the end of the month, or in the months its bank pays. A current account with a monthly fee takes it at the month’s end, unless the month met one of the bank’s waivers, such as enough paid in, enough kept in it, enough card purchases, or your age. The bank screen says how this month stands. Your credit card, on Cards, is the bank’s own, under its own name and terms. A statement comes on the 20th. Autopay pays the whole statement, or only the minimum if you choose, from the current account it’s set on, and Pay Card In Full pays the card off now, or as much as you have. Pay each statement in full and you’re charged no interest on it, unless the one before it wasn’t paid in full. If autopay can’t cover the payment, it comes back unpaid, and unless you paid the minimum by hand it counts as missed. Paying less than the minimum is late and costs a fee. Miss two payments in a row and the card is suspended; miss six, and it’s charged off to collections. When what’s in your current accounts and savings can’t clear the card, ask for help with payments: the bank freezes the interest, sets a payment that clears it in four years, and suspends the card until the first statement or due date after it’s paid off. Apply for a card at any bank in the country that issues one, yours or another. The bank looks at your credit record and what you have to repay from, and a card comes in about a week. You can hold one card from each bank. The day’s spending goes on the everyday card, and Make It The Everyday Card moves it to another. Statements, on a tab of their own, show every entry on each account you’ve held, month by month, with the balance after each. The Year adds up the interest, the tax taken off it and every fee. Alerts tell you when the current account ends a day under a line you set, when a payment goes over one, and when a statement is ready. How You Pay, on Payments And Cash, sets how the day’s spending is paid: on the debit card, the credit card or in cash. A purchase that way can’t cover isn’t made, and nothing is owed for it. Cash takes money out at a machine or the counter, for the bank’s fee, and pays cash back in. A card can be locked, so nothing new goes on it, and unlocked. Report one lost, stolen or damaged and it stops at once, and a new one with a new number follows. If a charge isn’t yours, report it in time, and the law decides what you carry and how fast the rest comes back. Where your bank runs one, an overdraft lets the account go below zero, with interest and any fee. A personal loan, on Borrowing, is repaid in equal monthly payments. You can open accounts at another bank where you live, and move to it. Next, “A Whole Life”: your own page, birthdays, and the end. The Details Interest is paid on the last day of the month, or in the months its bank pays: Germany’s at each quarter’s end, Japan’s in February and August, a Livret A in December. Where the country has the bank take tax off interest first, it does. Where a bank publishes no rate, the rate shown is estimated, and the screen says so. Deutsche Bank, Postbank and Berliner Sparkasse take a current account’s monthly fee at the quarter’s closing, and ING on the 1st of the month after. A card’s terms are its rate, or the range it prices a credit record within; any yearly fee; the minimum; the late fee; and the days to the due date, held to the law’s caps. Autopay also takes from easy-access savings at its account’s bank if you’ve linked savings. If they can’t cover all of it, the card charges a returned-payment fee for the miss, except on a US card when the late fee covers the same miss. If a statement isn’t paid in full, the interest built up to the day you cleared it comes on the next statement. Outside the US, paying less than the minimum also goes on your record and lowers your limit; in the US that happens only if the payment is still short at the next due date, thirty days on. Some banks, much of Europe’s among them, issue a card that takes the whole statement every month and can’t carry a balance. Where the bank’s card wasn’t found, its terms are estimated, and the screen says so. Each card has its own statement, due date and minimum, and your record looks at what’s used of all their limits together. Move the statement day and the due date moves with it. Ask for a higher limit or a lower one, and say whether the bank may raise it unasked. In the US, opt in to going over the limit or not. Close the card and it takes nothing new while what’s owed is paid down. Interest builds each day on what the card owes once a statement isn’t paid in full, and a statement paid in full keeps new spending interest-free. The rate is set by your record when the card opens, and held there. In the US a payment sixty days late ends any introductory rate, and where the card has a penalty rate it applies after 45 days’ notice, until six payments in a row are on time. Pay more than you owe and the card holds your credit; Send The Credit To My Account moves it back. Statements run quarter by quarter in Germany, whose banks close an account each quarter, and for the card they say what the statement asked and what paying only the minimum would take. Savings Covers The Current Account, turned on, pays what a current account can’t from easy-access savings at the same bank, for whatever that account pays: the day’s spending from the primary account, a card’s autopay from the account it’s set on, a loan’s payment from its repayment account. Standing orders move money into savings on a day each month, and a payment can be set for a date. Cash comes from your bank’s ATM, another bank’s, the counter, or in Britain the Post Office where your bank allows it, each for the bank’s own fee, and from the machines no more in a day than it lets out; another machine’s owner may add a fee of its own. A cash advance on the card costs its fee and draws its rate from the day it’s taken, with no grace period, and a payment to the card goes to the dearer balance first; in the US, only what you pay above the minimum does. The day’s groceries can be paid in cash. At a German bank that sets it, a savings account lets out €2,000 a month without notice, whether as cash, moved, or paid out for you, and no more. A new card after a report follows in the bank’s days, for its fee, and a card nearing its expiry has a new one sent ahead. Someone else may use a card, as often as the country’s official figures say it happens: the bank stops some of it and asks, and the rest shows up in an alert or on the statement. Where the bank does it, Move My Account To A New Number gives the current account a new number and a new debit card. Some cards open at an introductory rate for months, pay rewards on what goes on them, lend into your account for a money transfer, or put part of what purchases owe into a plan paid off in equal months, at the plan’s own fee or rate in place of the card’s interest; at Chase, a plan made while the card’s introductory rate runs costs nothing. A card applied for at another bank can take the balance of one you hold, for its fee and at its transfer rate, and the old card closes once it’s moved. A term deposit locks money away at a fixed rate for its term, and ends as its bank ends it unless you choose another way the bank allows, in time for any notice it needs: renewed for the same term, with any days’ grace to take it whole; moved into easy-access savings; or paid out. The bank writes before the end. Where the bank allows it, Break It Early costs the bank’s penalty, shown before you do it, and comes out of the deposit itself if the interest won’t cover it; some banks need notice first. Some banks let the interest be taken during the term, or charge for savings withdrawals past a free number a month. 20 banks move money into savings at the same bank as you spend, each its own way: the change to the next unit you choose, a set amount a purchase, or KakaoBank’s remainder of the day’s balance, on the bank’s own day and never into an overdraft. A choice the bank leaves to you, such as the unit or the amount, is yours to make before they start. An account left untouched goes inactive and then dormant, blocked until it’s reactivated, and after the country’s years its money goes to the state, to be claimed back. Where the country has a cooling-off period, a new account or card other than your primary can be canceled within it: in Korea a card alone, in Canada no card, in Britain no fixed-rate deposit, and in Mexico only one not yet used. A savings account can be opened, or closed with its money moved back. Where Germany’s monthly limit holds and it holds more than the month lets out, closing it gives three months’ notice, and it closes then. Past the bank’s free withdrawals in a month, each one out of savings costs its fee, a payment savings makes for you included. An account you kept in the country you lived in before Yale stays at its bank, shown in its own money with the dollars beside. It opens nothing new for you from abroad, takes its fee for a customer abroad and its card’s fee on purchases in dollars, closes on the day it says where its bank closes one, and sends and receives money only as a payment abroad, less its fees and the exchange. Closed with Close It, a German bank charges this month’s fee only for the days it’s been open, the figure said under the button before you press it. Its country takes its tax from the interest. On an F-1 the US doesn’t tax the interest; a US citizen reports it with the tax credited, and one whose accounts abroad passed $10,000 is told of the FBAR, and of Form 8938 past its own lines, with the return. An overdraft is arranged to a limit, with interest and any fee, or paid item by item with a fee for each, its interest charged on the 1st or when the account closes. A US bank won’t pay an everyday debit card purchase into an overdraft unless you’ve opted in. An account left below zero past any arranged limit for sixty days is closed (in France, two months after the bank gives notice): the bank charges off what it can’t recover, with the interest and fees owed to the day, the debt goes to collections and onto your record, a standing order from or into the account ends with it, and your money moves to another bank. In the US the bank reports such a closing to ChexSystems for five years, and the next bank may turn you away (most do while the debt is unpaid, about half once it’s paid), leaving a second-chance account with a monthly fee and no overdraft. Pay It, under In Collections, pays the debt down. A French bank writes after a month past your overdraft and offers a loan for it at three: Take The Loan takes it, and once the account is back within its limit the sixty days start again. A personal loan payment the account can’t cover in full takes what’s there and goes on your record as missed, with the bank’s fee where it charges one, and the rest stays owed. When you move to another bank, your card stays with the bank that issued it, as loans and term deposits do. The bank’s name, each account’s name and every line on a statement open where they stand: the bank into the accounts it holds for you and the latest money through them, and a line into the accounts it moved between and the day it went through.

The Player Profile is you as an actor. Open it from Player Profile in the menu, or by pressing your name at the menu’s foot. It holds your twelve abilities: Emotional Depth, Presence, Listening And Responsiveness, Imagination, Focus, Memory, Adaptability, Composure, Vocal Expression, Physical Expression, and beside them Discipline and Stamina. Each runs from 0 to 100, and opens onto what it is, what grows it and where it matters. Beside them are your 17 techniques, the skills the school trains, each mastered at 90. The traits you chose at the start are there too, each opening onto what it changes. Four things make you better, and they aren’t the same. Your abilities are you: how you listen, how you hold your nerve. Your techniques are skills a class trains. Your genre knowledge is how well you know a kind of play or film. Practice is work you set yourself, outside class. Classes, coursework, rehearsals and nights on stage grow what they train, up to the level the classes reach. Practice takes you further, all the way to the top. In the Practice column, press the exercise beside an ability or a technique, such as Comic Timing, pick a tier and a focus, and press Practice An Hour. Before the hour, it says about how much it will add, and why. The same growth from the same work again on the same day gives half, whether it’s a class, a night or an exercise, and past 4 hours a day practice stops counting. A technique past 90 improves no result, because no work asks more of it. Finished work, a showing, a night or a self-tape, is judged on what your teacher looks for: believability, interpretation, connection, execution, consistency and responsiveness, as the work asks. Each piece says what held it back, and by how much, and Your Work So Far says what your work shows most and what keeps holding it back. Under Genres, Study It reads a play of a genre or watches a film of the kind. Every genre also has a practice brief, an original scene written for SLATE: Open Its Brief to read where and when it is, who the two of you are, what you want, what’s in the way and where it turns. On the brief, Break It Down, Learn The Lines and Rehearse It On Your Feet prepare it. Read It This Way chooses a reading, and you choose how to play it; from then on Work It Through works on it as on a scene. Read With chooses who reads opposite you: a recording, or a classmate who learns it as you rehearse together. Then Tape It. A self-tape asks you to know its genre too: the better of what you’ve studied and what you’ve worked in it, complete at 90. A genre you’ve never met costs at most 25% of a tape’s interpretation. What To Work On forecasts what 20 sessions of practice, a day apart, would bring on each thing your work depends on. It’s a forecast, not a result, and it says what it assumes. What finishing a piece’s own preparation would bring is shown apart from it, so you can tell how ready a piece is now from how ready it could be. How you play a scene can suit it or go against it, by what the scene is: its genre, its reader, its tones, its turns and the reading you’ve chosen. A way that suits the scene is easier to make work and quicker to rehearse, and one against it is harder. Either can make the better tape if it plays to what you do well, and the tape says which. The tape with your reader is what a casting office would see. A Strong one earns a callback: a harder version with a note on how to play it, answered only by a tape played that way. Loving a genre makes a rehearsal or a night of a play of its kind a better day, but never better work. Next, “Cover, Benefits And Returns” opens Papers: your health cover, benefits and tax returns. The Details Discipline grows with each day you practice and each call you keep, and it makes what classes, nights, steps and practice grow count a little more, or less. Practice doesn’t train Stamina: it’s your fitness, built by Work Out, under Look After Yourself, and by a class that trains the body as hard, Movement Invention or stage combat. Memory makes learning lines go further, imagination the table work and a brief’s breakdown, focus getting to know a scene, listening an evening rehearsing with a partner or a reader and a brief rehearsed against its reader, and adaptability a revision after a note and a director’s note on the blocking. Which abilities count most depends on the piece: a long text asks memory, a lot riding on it composure, little of a partner to play off presence, many beats discipline, a grave piece emotional depth, a comic one focus, and an invented world imagination. A night in front of an audience asks presence, memory, composure and discipline of its own, and stamina by how long and how hard it is. With every ability at full, every technique mastered, the preparation complete and you rested and well, a piece comes to 100. A self-tape counts as practice in its genre by how prepared it was: one taped cold counts 15% of a prepared one. What a genre asks of an actor, where research established it, is a technique its brief stages, shown before you begin. Every genre’s scenes are played in its own register, one or two concerns such as feeling held in, comic rhythm or belief in what isn’t there. The Genre Asks names them on its brief, each pulls the tape toward what it asks, and a tape held back by an ability one of them asks for says so. What To Work On can name something other than what held your last tape back most, and says when two things are about as useful. It says what its forecast assumes of your tapes and their genres, naming any it treats as if fully prepared, and whether preparation, training or both deserve your attention now. Asked again, it says how long its advice has stood, what has moved since and what moved it, and what to do next. A tape is judged beat by beat: each beat’s rehearsal, how far into the tape it falls, where stamina tells, and a slip in the beat before, which composure and adaptability help you recover from. Your own tape is yours, whoever reads with you.

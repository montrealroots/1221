Drama and film in which conventional plot, characterization and logic are ignored or distorted to convey the irrationality of existence and humanity's isolation in a meaningless world, as in the post-war Theater of the Absurd. What it asks of an actor. Absurdist drama, Beckett above all, asks actors for exact control of timing, rhythm and timed silence, and in Waiting for Godot for the cross-talk and clown business of vaudeville and music hall. Why it adds no requirement. What the research found (Comic Timing, Physical Comedy) belongs to Beckett’s plays (Waiting for Godot’s cross-talk and clown business), not to the genre as a whole, so the genre adds no requirement of its own. What the research says: The study guide, from a search summary: Beckett 'places strong emphasis on the rhythmic qualities of his plays, and like a composer, he is conscious of the importance of precisely timed silence'; his plays put actors in very difficult positions, some unable to speak and others unable to move; one actress says the voice must concentrate on 'timing, rhythm and the musicality of the text'. Wikipedia, from a search summary, says Godot borrows from 'the cross-talk tradition of the vaudeville stage, the burlesque, the music hall, and the movies of Charlie Chaplin, Buster Keaton, Laurel and Hardy', with hat games, dropped trousers and similar business. The Northern Star blog says Godot owes much of its form and rhythm to vaudeville and clown acts. (from a search summary; low confidence). The study guide’s PDF couldn’t be read as text, so the quotations come from a search engine’s summary of it, not from a direct reading. Nothing found covers 'holding comedy and despair together' as an acting demand in so many words.
